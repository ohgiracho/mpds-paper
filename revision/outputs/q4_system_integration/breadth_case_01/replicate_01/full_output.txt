Context: [User's Experimental Situation]
- Background: Aqueous batteries are attractive for safe and low-cost energy storage, but their practical voltage, lifetime, and power are constrained by water and by the electrode-electrolyte interfaces.
- Thermodynamic and kinetic bottleneck: The narrow thermodynamic stability window of water promotes hydrogen and oxygen evolution. Kinetic suppression can widen the apparent operating window, but trace electrolysis can persist during cycling.
- Interfacial bottleneck: Small parasitic currents can shift the local interfacial pH, accelerate electrode-material dissolution, and progressively destabilize both interfaces.
- Transport trade-off: Concentrated and hybrid electrolytes can reduce water activity, yet higher viscosity, lower ionic conductivity, and larger desolvation barriers can compromise fast interfacial transport.
- Core question: What electrolyte and interfacial-control strategy can jointly limit gas evolution, pH drift, dissolution, and transport resistance without relying on a single kinetically expanded stability window?
- Goals:
    1) Define a coupled electrolyte-interface mechanism rather than an isolated additive effect.
    2) Preserve rapid ion transfer while suppressing cumulative parasitic reactions.
    3) Specify operando or post-mortem measurements that could falsify the proposed mechanism.

[Battery-system specialist (Scientist A) - Round 1]
The core of the problem is a fundamental misunderstanding that has permeated the field. Many researchers are chasing a wider electrochemical stability window (ESW) as a singular goal, treating it as a static property of the bulk electrolyte [ID: 42, 416]. This is a superficial approach. The thermodynamic limit of water at 1.23 V is a reality of chemistry; simply increasing salt concentration to gain a few hundred millivolts on a linear sweep voltammogram is not a paradigm shift, especially when such measurements often mask underlying, parasitic currents that lead to failure over practical timescales [ID: 59].

The true challenge is not in the bulk electrolyte but at the dynamic electrode-electrolyte interface. The enemy is not water itself, but uncontrolled *water activity* at the electrode surface, particularly the water molecules within the cation's primary solvation sheath that are delivered directly to the interface [ID: 251, 353]. It is this interfacial water that fuels hydrogen evolution, triggers local pH shifts, and ultimately leads to the dissolution of cathode materials like vanadium oxides [ID: 65, 90, 304] or the structural collapse of hosts like Prussian blue analogues [ID: 83]. Focusing solely on bulk properties like high salt concentration often leads to impractical electrolytes with high viscosity, poor ionic conductivity, and prohibitive cost, without addressing the root cause of interfacial instability [ID: 281, 293].

My proposed solution is a two-pronged strategy focused on engineering the nanoscale environment where the chemistry actually happens: the ion's immediate vicinity and the electrode surface.

**1. Targeted Solvation Sheath Engineering:**

We must move beyond the brute-force approach of "Water-in-Salt" and instead precisely control the cation's primary solvation sheath. The goal is to competitively and selectively displace reactive water molecules from the ion's inner coordination sphere before they reach the electrode. This is achieved not just by high salt concentration, but by introducing a molecular species with a stronger affinity for the cation than water.

*   **For Zinc Systems:** The addition of organic solvents with a high Gutmann donor number, such as dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO), has been shown to preferentially coordinate with Zn²⁺, displacing water from the solvation shell [ID: 5]. This directly reduces the local water activity at the anode surface, suppressing water decomposition.
*   **For Lithium Systems:** The introduction of a carefully selected second salt, creating a "Water-in-Bisalt" [ID: 1, 4] or even a non-Li co-salt [ID: 8], can fundamentally alter the Li⁺ solvation structure. These co-solvents or co-salts disrupt the hydrogen-bonding network of water and compete for a place in the Li⁺ sheath, effectively "drying" the cation before it reaches the negative electrode [ID: 329].

This is a more elegant and efficient way to control water's reactivity than simply overwhelming the system with salt. It directly targets the primary culprit: solvated water [ID: 251, 303].

**2. *In-Situ* Formation of a Robust, Selective Solid Electrolyte Interphase (SEI):**

Kinetic stability can only be achieved through the formation of a passivating interphase, a concept we successfully transplanted from non-aqueous to aqueous systems [ID: 12]. A wide ESW is meaningless if the electrodes are not protected. The electrolyte must be designed for the *sacrificial decomposition* of a specific component—either the anion or a co-solvent—to build a stable, ion-selective SEI.

This SEI must be:
*   **Inorganic-Rich and Dense:** The decomposition of anions like bis(trifluoromethanesulfonyl)imide (TFSI⁻) is known to form a robust, LiF- or ZnF₂-rich interphase [ID: 12, 60, 78]. This inorganic backbone provides mechanical strength to suppress dendrites and is a superior barrier to water and electron tunneling compared to the soluble or porous hydroxides formed from water reduction [ID: 88].
*   **Ionically Conductive:** The SEI must allow efficient transport of the charge carrier (e.g., Li⁺, Zn²⁺) while blocking solvent molecules and counter-ions [ID: 5, 22].
*   **Self-Healing/Dynamically Stable:** The electrolyte must contain a reservoir of the SEI-forming species to repair any mechanical damage to the interphase during cycling [ID: 207].

This strategy decouples the stability of the electrode from the bulk electrolyte. The electrode is no longer in direct contact with water; it operates within a protective niche created by the electrolyte itself.

**Falsification and Verification:**

Any claims based on this strategy must be substantiated with rigorous mechanistic evidence. Flashy cycle numbers are insufficient.

1.  **Prove the Solvation Sheath Modification:** Use Raman and NMR spectroscopy to show a decrease in the signal for water coordinated to the cation and an increase in the signal for the competing solvent or anion coordination [ID: 78, 219]. Corroborate this with molecular dynamics simulations showing a reduced water coordination number in the first solvation shell [ID: 2, 5, 38].
2.  **Characterize the SEI:** Use XPS depth profiling to demonstrate that the interphase is composed of anion or solvent decomposition products (e.g., -CF₃, -SO₂, LiF, ZnF₂) and not just metal oxides/hydroxides [ID: 12, 60]. Use cryo-TEM to visualize its morphology and density.
3.  **Quantify Parasitic Reactions:** Employ *in-situ* techniques like Online Electrochemical Mass Spectrometry (ECMS) to quantify the reduction in H₂ evolution compared to a standard electrolyte [ID: 262]. This provides direct, undeniable evidence of suppressed water decomposition.
4.  **Measure Ion Transport:** Determine the cation transference number and use computational methods to show a reduced desolvation energy barrier at the newly formed interface [ID: 202]. This demonstrates that the SEI is not simply a resistive blockage but a functional, selective transport medium.

Without this level of fundamental proof, any report of "improved stability" is merely an observation, not science. We must build our understanding from the interface up, not from the bulk down.
