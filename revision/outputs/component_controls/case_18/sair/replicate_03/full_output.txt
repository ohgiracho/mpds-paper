[FINAL SYNTHESIS]
To achieve stable, high-performance potassium-ion storage, metal sulfide nanoparticles must be encapsulated within a hierarchical structure that mitigates mechanical stress from potassiation, ensures rapid ion and electron transport, and immobilizes soluble potassium polysulfide intermediates. The recommended framework involves aggregating multi-yolk-shell primary particles into macroporous microspheres. This design addresses the key structural, transport, and chemical stability bottlenecks inherent to using metal sulfide conversion materials for potassium-ion battery (KIB) anodes.

### Recommended Framework: Hierarchical Microspheres of Multi-Yolk-Shell Nanoparticles

The proposed architecture consists of three engineered levels: (1) ultrafine metal sulfide nanoparticles as the active material "yolks"; (2) a mesoporous, N,S-doped hollow carbon nanosphere (NS-HCNS) as the primary particle "shell"; and (3) a macroporous microsphere formed by the aggregation of these primary yolk-shell particles.

#### 1. Primary Particle Design: Multi-Yolk@NS-HCNS

The fundamental building block is a multi-yolk-shell particle designed to manage stress and confine reaction byproducts at the nanoscale.

*   **Active Material (Yolks):** Iron disulfide (FeS₂) is a well-supported choice for the active material. It offers high theoretical capacity and is composed of earth-abundant, low-cost elements (Scientist A [ID: 149], Scientist A [ID: 247]). Its effectiveness in nanostructured KIB anodes has been demonstrated (Scientist A [ID: 348], Scientist A [ID: 470]). The FeS₂ should be synthesized as ultrafine nanoparticles (<10 nm) to maximize electrochemically active sites, shorten K-ion diffusion pathways, and improve intrinsic tolerance to pulverization (Scientist A [ID: 93], Scientist A [ID: 111]).

*   **Void Engineering for Stress Accommodation:** The large K-ion induces severe volume expansion in conversion-type anodes, leading to mechanical failure. A yolk-shell structure, which features an engineered void between the active material "yolks" and the carbon "shell," is a highly effective strategy to accommodate this strain (Scientist A [ID: 104], Scientist B [ID: 43]). The internal void provides buffer space, preventing expansion-induced fracture of the conductive shell and preserving electrical contact over repeated cycles (Scientist A [ID: 57], Scientist B [ID: 394]). A multi-yolk configuration, where several nanoparticles are confined within one shell, offers superior active material loading and structural stability compared to a single-yolk design (Scientist A [ID: 57]). This configuration enhances density by packing more active material into each primary particle.

*   **Shell Engineering for Transport and Polysulfide Confinement:** The hollow carbon nanosphere shell must be multifunctional.
    1.  **Chemical Anchoring:** The carbon shell should be co-doped with nitrogen (N) and sulfur (S). Heteroatom doping creates polar sites (e.g., pyridinic-N, C–S bonds) on the carbon surface that can chemically anchor soluble potassium polysulfide (KₓSᵧ) intermediates (Scientist A [ID: 2], Scientist B [ID: 17]). The dissolution and shuttling of these intermediates is a primary failure mechanism in KIBs (Scientist A [ID: 49], Scientist A [ID: 107]). While this anchoring strategy is proven for Li- and Na-ion systems, its efficacy for K-polysulfides is an inference. However, it is chemically plausible that the Lewis acid-base interactions between polar sites on the carbon and the K-polysulfide species will be analogous, supplementing the physical confinement provided by the hollow shell and suppressing capacity fade (Scientist A [ID: 348]).
    2.  **Ion Transport:** The shell must be mesoporous to allow unimpeded access of the K-ion-containing electrolyte to the encapsulated FeS₂ yolks (Scientist B [ID: 97], Scientist B [ID: 211], Scientist B [ID: 381]). A nonporous or purely microporous shell would create a significant transport barrier, limiting rate capability. There is an inherent trade-off between shell porosity, which benefits ion transport, and mechanical strength; the pore structure must be optimized to ensure sufficient transport without compromising the shell's ability to withstand volume expansion.

#### 2. Secondary Particle Design: Macroporous Microsphere Assembly

The primary yolk-shell particles must be aggregated into microspheres in a way that preserves electrolyte access to the core of the aggregate, addressing the secondary particle transport bottleneck.

*   **Interconnected Pore Network:** To prevent dense packing that chokes ion transport, a sacrificial templating agent, such as sodium chloride (NaCl) crystals, should be incorporated into the slurry during the assembly process (Scientist A [ID: 29]). Subsequent washing removes the NaCl, leaving behind an interconnected network of macropores within the microsphere. This network ensures electrolyte penetration throughout the secondary particle, making all primary particles electrochemically accessible.

*   **Density and Performance Trade-off:** Introducing macropores necessarily lowers the tap density of the final material, creating a trade-off against volumetric energy density. However, this is a required compromise, as the interior of a non-porous microsphere would become "dead volume" at practical rates, leading to poor active material utilization (Scientist A [ID: 29]). The optimal level of macroporosity, controlled by the mass ratio of the NaCl template to the primary particles, must be determined empirically to balance the competing demands of high rate capability and high volumetric energy density.

#### 3. Synthesis and Process Integration

A multi-step synthesis is recommended to achieve this hierarchical architecture. The final assembly is compatible with the user's spray-drying equipment when followed by a thermal treatment step.

1.  **Synthesis of Primary Particles (FeS₂@NS-HCNS):** A robust method involves sacrificial templating. Silica (SiO₂) nanospheres can be coated with a nitrogen-containing polymer (e.g., polydopamine) mixed with an iron precursor (Scientist B [ID: 2]). The core-shell particles are then pyrolyzed and sulfurized to form FeS₂ nanoparticles within an N-doped carbon shell (Scientist A [ID: 45], Scientist B [ID: 469]). Etching the SiO₂ template (e.g., with NaOH) creates the internal void, yielding FeS₂@NS-HCNS yolk-shell particles (Scientist B [ID: 43], Scientist B [ID: 91]). Achieving effective N,S co-doping requires careful optimization of precursor choice and thermal processing conditions to ensure dopant incorporation without compromising the carbon structure.
2.  **Assembly of Microspheres:** The synthesized FeS₂@NS-HCNS primary particles are dispersed in a slurry with NaCl crystals (Scientist A [ID: 29]). This slurry is then fed into a **spray dryer** to form aggregated microspheres. Following spray drying, a separate **calcination step** under an inert atmosphere is required. This high-temperature treatment sinters the carbon contact points between primary particles, ensuring mechanical integrity and electrical conductivity throughout the microsphere (Scientist A [ID: 22], Scientist A [ID: 101]). After cooling, the microspheres are washed with water to remove the NaCl template, yielding the final macroporous, hierarchical product.

#### 4. Electrolyte Formulation

For metal sulfide anodes in KIBs, ether-based electrolytes (e.g., based on dimethoxyethane, DME) are recommended over carbonate-based ones. They tend to form a more stable solid electrolyte interphase (SEI) and can help mitigate polysulfide issues, leading to higher initial Coulombic efficiency and better cycling stability (Scientist A [ID: 284], Scientist A [ID: 348]).
