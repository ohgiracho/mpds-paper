To improve the performance of sodium-ion battery (SIB) anodes based on Prussian blue analogue (PBA)-derived hollow sulfide nanocubes and reduced graphene oxide (rGO), a synthesis strategy must be employed that creates a structurally robust, three-dimensional (3D) hierarchical composite. This architecture should leverage the high capacity of the sulfide while mitigating its structural instability, and exploit the conductivity of rGO without creating ion transport barriers. The most defensible framework involves a two-step process that combines spray pyrolysis to form composite microspheres followed by a controlled sulfidation to generate the final active material.

### Analysis of Core Challenges

A successful anode design must overcome four interconnected material and process challenges:

1.  **Structural and Electrical Instability:** The large volume changes inherent to the conversion reaction of metal sulfides during sodiation and desodiation lead to pulverization of the active material. This process causes the sulfide particles to lose electrical contact with each other and the current collector, resulting in rapid capacity fade and poor cycling stability Scientist B [ID: 133], Scientist B [ID: 246], Scientist B [ID: 468].
2.  **Interfacial Decoupling:** The composite's integrity relies on the adhesion between the sulfide nanocubes and the rGO matrix. During cycling, mechanical stress from the sulfide's volume expansion can overcome the interfacial forces, causing the components to separate. This decoupling leads to the disintegration of the conductive network and the electrical isolation of the active material, rendering it inactive. **Inference:** This is a common failure mode in composite electrodes employing high-volume-change conversion materials, where the flexible carbon matrix must remain physically and electrically coupled to the active phase to ensure long-term stability Scientist B [ID: 112], Scientist B [ID: 279].
3.  **Transport Bottlenecks:** While rGO provides a conductive scaffold, its two-dimensional (2D) nature creates two potential transport issues. First, rGO sheets tend to aggregate and restack into a graphitic structure, which eliminates the porosity required for electrolyte infiltration and ion access to the active material Scientist B [ID: 86], Scientist B [ID: 106]. Second, if rGO is simply used to coat the sulfide particles, it can form an impermeable layer that blocks the porous walls of the nanocubes, severely limiting Na-ion diffusion to the electrochemically active sites. **Inference:** A conformal coating of rGO, while beneficial for conductivity, may hinder ion transport if not engineered to be porous or if it obstructs the inherent porosity of the active material Scientist B [ID: 473].
4.  **Process-Specific Constraints:** The spray pyrolysis and sulfidation steps must be carefully controlled to achieve the desired architecture. The temperature during spray pyrolysis, for instance, must be sufficient to evaporate the solvent and initiate GO reduction but controlled to prevent uncontrolled aggregation of the sheets, which would disrupt the formation of a uniform composite. ### Recommended Framework: Two-Step Spray Pyrolysis and Gaseous Sulfidation

A two-step strategy that decouples the structural assembly from the chemical conversion is the most defensible approach. This method first uses spray pyrolysis to create well-defined composite microspheres and then employs a controlled sulfidation to form the final porous, hierarchical anode material.

**1. Step 1: Spray Pyrolysis of PBA/GO Precursor Microspheres**

The process begins by preparing a stable, homogeneous aqueous dispersion of pre-synthesized PBA nanocube templates and graphene oxide (GO) sheets. PBA nanocubes are effective templates for forming porous or hollow metal sulfides Scientist A [ID: 1], Scientist A [ID: 2], Scientist A [ID: 15], Scientist A [ID: 72]. GO is used as the precursor for rGO due to its excellent dispersibility in water, which is enabled by its oxygen-containing functional groups Scientist B [ID: 27].

This dispersion is atomized into fine droplets and passed through a heated reactor. Inside each droplet, the rapid evaporation of water facilitates the self-assembly of GO sheets and PBA nanocubes into a composite microsphere. This process promotes a uniform distribution of the PBA templates within a GO matrix. The reactor temperature should be sufficient to evaporate the solvent and partially reduce the GO to rGO but low enough to preserve the crystalline structure of the PBA template. Temperatures in the range of 200–400 °C are typically effective for initial thermal reduction of GO without causing excessive damage to precursor structures Scientist B [ID: 10], Scientist B [ID: 293].

**2. Step 2: Controlled Gaseous Sulfidation of Composite Microspheres**

The PBA/rGO composite microspheres obtained from spray pyrolysis are subsequently subjected to a controlled high-temperature sulfidation. *   **Porous Sulfide Formation:** The PBA nanocubes embedded within the rGO matrix are converted into hollow and/or porous metal sulfide nanocubes. *   **Final Reduction and 3D Network Fixation:** The high temperature of the sulfidation process completes the reduction of any remaining oxygen functional groups on the GO, yielding a highly conductive rGO network Scientist B [ID: 32]. ### Architectural Advantages and Resolution of Bottlenecks

This two-step approach creates a synergistic composite that effectively addresses the core challenges:

*   **Mitigation of Pore Blocking:** By forming the composite with solid PBA precursors *before* sulfidation, the rGO sheets are physically propped apart into a stable 3D arrangement. The subsequent *in situ* conversion generates the porosity within the sulfide nanocubes after the rGO scaffold is already established. This strategy avoids the primary failure mode where rGO sheets are applied to an already-porous material and block the pore entrances. **Inference:** Because the rGO scaffold is established around solid PBA templates before their conversion, the subsequent creation of porosity within the sulfides is less likely to be obstructed. The rGO sheets are already locked in place, preventing them from collapsing onto and blocking the newly formed porous surfaces of the sulfide nanocubes.
*   **Structural and Electrical Integrity:** The resulting 3D rGO network serves as a flexible and highly conductive scaffold. The highly conductive rGO sheets provide continuous, long-range pathways for efficient electron transport throughout the electrode.

**Uncertainty:** The optimal annealing temperature and duration for the sulfidation step represent a critical trade-off that defines a narrow processing window. *   **Insufficient Temperature:** A temperature that is too low or a reaction time that is too short will result in the incomplete conversion of PBA to the metal sulfide. *   **Excessive Temperature:** A temperature that is too high can cause sintering of the newly formed sulfide nanocubes, leading to particle growth and the collapse of the hollow, porous morphology. The electrochemical consequence is a rapid loss of capacity, particularly at high current densities.

Navigating this trade-off requires systematic experimental optimization to identify the conditions that maximize sulfide conversion while retaining the hierarchical, porous architecture of the composite.
