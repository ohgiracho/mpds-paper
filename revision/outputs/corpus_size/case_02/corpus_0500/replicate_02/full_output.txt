### Diagnosis of the Core Problem

Your experimental approach—using spray drying to create multicomponent hollow microspheres—is sound in principle. However, the bottleneck you're hitting is predictable. A simple hollow shell made of a pure (or even mixed) metal chalcogenide is fundamentally flawed for sodium-ion storage. Here's why:

1.  **Inherent Brittleness and Low Conductivity:** Most metal sulfides and selenides are semiconductors with poor electronic conductivity [ID: 3, 24]. During the large volume changes associated with sodiation, a brittle, monolithic shell will inevitably crack and pulverize. This leads to a catastrophic loss of electrical contact between particles and with the current collector, causing rapid capacity fade.
2.  **Inefficient Void Utilization:** If the shell is too thick or non-porous, the internal void becomes "dead space." The Na+ ions can only access the outer surface, and the interior surface area is wasted. This negates the primary benefit of the hollow architecture and kills your volumetric energy density.
3.  **Uncontrolled Phase Segregation:** In a multicomponent Mo/Ni system, simple co-precipitation and annealing can lead to large, segregated domains of different sulfide phases. This non-uniformity creates stress points and inconsistent electrochemical activity, accelerating failure.

Your problem isn't the hollow sphere concept itself, but the *composition and internal structure of the shell*. You're building a fragile eggshell when you need a reinforced, porous composite.
