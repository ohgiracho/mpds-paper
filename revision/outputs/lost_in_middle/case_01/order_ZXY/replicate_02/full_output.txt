The main design levers are CAM–SE coverage degree, how limited LPSCl is allocated between surface coverage and interstitial filling, SE particle-size distribution, and mechanofusion processing conditions within this fixed composite.

- Transport bottleneck:
 The central question is how limited LPSCl should be split between (i) surface coverage on the polycrystalline NCM811 secondary particles and (ii) interstitial filling between particles. Both the surface-coverage fraction and the filler fraction must remain LPSCl. CAM–SE coverage degree and LPSCl particle-size allocation jointly affect ionic continuity, electronic accessibility, and microstructural tortuosity. The optimal balance is not known in advance and must be reasoned out.

 The original polycrystalline NCM811 secondary-microsphere morphology must remain intact during mechanofusion. The answer should preserve this fixed polycrystalline secondary-particle platform rather than replacing it with single-crystal NCM, alternative cathode morphologies, new active-material platforms, or alternative electrolyte chemistries.

Excellent. This is a well-defined, practical problem that gets to the heart of composite cathode engineering for all-solid-state batteries (ASSBs). Too much of the literature focuses on novel materials while ignoring the fundamental challenge of arranging them effectively in a high-loading, high-density electrode.

### Diagnosis

Your core issue is the classic trade-off triangle in composite electrodes: ionic conductivity, electronic conductivity, and volumetric energy density. At >85 wt% active material, you have less than 15 wt% to allocate between your solid electrolyte (SE) and conductive additive (CA). Every volume percent given to the SE to improve ionic pathways is taken from the CA or the NCM811, potentially compromising electronic pathways or energy density.

The central question of allocating a *limited* amount of LPSCl between surface coverage and interstitial filling is critical. A flawed geometric arrangement will create performance bottlenecks, regardless of how good the individual components are. My primary concern with your setup is the use of mechanofusion. It's a high-energy, blunt-force method. While it can ensure dense packing, precisely controlling the final distribution of SE particles—differentiating between a "coating" and "filler"—is exceptionally difficult. The process itself tends to pulverize materials, which could unintentionally create a distribution of particle sizes. Your task is to turn this challenge into a controlled strategy.

Based on my analysis of the field, a simple binary choice between "all coating" or "all interstitial" is a false dichotomy. Both extremes are fundamentally flawed.

1.  **The Pure Surface-Coverage Fallacy:** Attempting to use only fine LPSCl particles to coat the NCM811 secondary spheres would be detrimental. While it might maximize the initial CAM-SE contact area, it would also coat the conductive additive particles, effectively strangling the electronic percolation network [ID: 421, 463]. This would lead to massive electronic resistance. Furthermore, the interstitial voids between the large, coated NCM811 spheres would be poorly filled, resulting in low packing density and poor mechanical integrity, creating new voids upon cycling [ID: 5].

2.  **The Pure Interstitial-Filling Fallacy:** Using only large LPSCl particles to fill the voids between NCM811 spheres is equally inefficient. This approach minimizes the CAM-SE interfacial area, creating an "ion-starved" condition. Lithium ions would have to travel long, tortuous paths across the NCM811 surface to find a limited number of contact points. This creates a severe diffusive limitation, leading to high polarization and poor rate capability, especially in thick electrodes [ID: 31, 157]. You would underutilize the NCM811 active material.

### Proposed Solution: A Hierarchical, Mixed-Particle-Size Allocation of LPSCl

The most robust and practical strategy within your constraints is to engineer a **hierarchical microstructure using a bimodal or multi-modal particle size distribution for the LPSCl electrolyte.** This isn't about simply mixing powders; it's about assigning specific roles to different particle size fractions to simultaneously optimize ionic, electronic, and mechanical networks.

This approach is directly supported by experimental evidence showing that cathode composites with mixed particle-sized solid electrolytes exhibit higher specific capacitance than those with uniform-sized electrolytes [ID: 345].

Here is the breakdown of the strategy:

**1. Fine LPSCl Fraction for Interfacial Contact:**
A portion of your LPSCl should be processed into a fine powder (e.g., sub-micron). During mechanofusion, these fine particles will have a higher surface energy and will preferentially adhere to the surfaces of the larger NCM811 and conductive additive particles.
*   **Function:** This creates a high density of ionically active contact points across the NCM811 surface, drastically reducing the Li-ion diffusion length within the active material particle itself. This directly tackles the diffusive kinetic limitations that dominate at high active material ratios [ID: 31]. It increases the active surface area and improves active material utilization [ID: 252].
*   **Control:** The amount must be carefully controlled. Too much will begin to isolate the conductive additive network, increasing electronic resistance [ID: 421].

**2. Coarse LPSCl Fraction for Interstitial Scaffolding and Ionic Highways:**
The remaining, larger fraction of LPSCl (e.g., micron-sized) will serve a different purpose. These particles are too large to form a conformal coating and will primarily fill the interstitial voids between the NCM811 secondary spheres.
*   **Function:** This fraction accomplishes three critical goals:
    *   **High Packing Density:** It efficiently fills the large voids, maximizing the overall tap density of the composite cathode, which is essential for volumetric energy density.
    *   **Low-Tortuosity Ionic Network:** These large particles form a continuous, percolating "ionic highway" that bridges the NCM811 particles. This provides a fast path for Li-ions to travel deep into the thick electrode, complementing the local transport provided by the finer particles [ID: 157].
    *   **Mechanical Stability:** This robust, packed structure helps accommodate the chemo-mechanical stresses from the NCM811 volume changes during cycling, reducing the likelihood of delamination and contact loss [ID: 252].

### Practical Implementation and Caveats

Your primary lever is the **mechanofusion process itself.** You need to optimize the energy, duration, and ball-milling media to control the final particle size distribution of the LPSCl.
*   A short, lower-energy milling step could be used to create the fine LPSCl fraction first.
*   Then, a subsequent, carefully controlled co-milling of the NCM811, conductive additive, fine LPSCl, and coarse LPSCl could achieve the desired hierarchical structure without completely pulverizing the coarse LPSCl or the NCM811 secondary particles. This is a significant process engineering challenge.

Even with this optimized geometry, you must acknowledge the inherent limitations of your fixed platform:

*   **Interfacial Reactivity:** You are using a bare NCM811 and a sulfide electrolyte. No matter how perfect the contact, an undesirable interfacial layer will form due to the chemical and electrochemical instability between the oxide cathode and sulfide electrolyte [ID: 339, 415]. This will inevitably lead to impedance growth over cycling. Strategies like ALD coatings (e.g., ZrO₂ [ID: 387], LiNbO₃ [ID: 101]) are known to suppress this, but are outside your scope. Your geometric solution mitigates kinetic problems but does not solve this fundamental chemical instability.
*   **Polycrystalline Degradation:** Polycrystalline NCM811 is prone to intergranular cracking from anisotropic volume changes, which creates new, unpassivated surfaces and accelerates degradation [ID: 22, 213]. While a well-packed composite can provide some mechanical confinement, it cannot eliminate this intrinsic failure mode of the active material itself. Single-crystal cathodes are a known solution to this, but again, fall outside your specified constraints [ID: 152, 286].

### Conclusion

For your fixed NCM811/LPSCl/CA composite, do not pursue a monolithic coating or simple interstitial filling. The most scientifically sound approach is to engineer a **hierarchical composite using a mixed-particle-size distribution of LPSCl.** Use fine LPSCl to establish a rich network of interfacial contacts for fast local kinetics, and use coarse LPSCl to build a dense, low-tortuosity ionic scaffold for efficient long-range transport and mechanical robustness. This strategy offers the most plausible balance between ionic continuity, electronic accessibility, and packing density. However, be prepared for long-term degradation originating from the fundamental interfacial reactivity of your chosen materials, which this geometric optimization alone cannot prevent.
