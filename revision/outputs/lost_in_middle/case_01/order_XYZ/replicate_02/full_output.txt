### Diagnosis of the Core Problem

The challenge you've outlined is fundamental to fabricating any high-loading composite electrode, but it's acutely severe in all-solid-state systems. In a liquid electrolyte, we take for granted that the electrolyte will wet every nook and cranny. Here, we are forced to build ionic and electronic pathways particle by particle. With over 85 wt% active material, the available volume for both the solid electrolyte (LPSCl) and the conductive additive is critically low. This creates a direct conflict:

1.  **Ionic Isolation:** Insufficient or poorly distributed LPSCl creates "ionically dead" zones on the NCM811 particle surfaces. This leads to inhomogeneous lithiation, high tortuosity for Li-ion pathways, and massive polarization at any meaningful C-rate [ID: 5, 157]. The result is a battery that only works on paper at C/20.
2.  **Electronic Isolation:** Conversely, if we are overzealous in coating the NCM811 particles with LPSCl to ensure ionic contact, we risk electrically isolating them from the conductive additive network. This is especially problematic with sulfide electrolytes, where the interface with carbon additives can itself be a source of instability and resistance [ID: 20]. A high electronic resistance is just as fatal as a high ionic one.
3.  **Chemo-mechanical Degradation:** Polycrystalline NCM811 is notorious for microcracking due to anisotropic volume changes during cycling [ID: 213, 385]. In a solid-state system, these cracks open up new, unpassivated surfaces. If there is no SE within these cracks, they become ionically inaccessible, leading to capacity fade. If the electrode structure cannot accommodate this stress, you get delamination and contact loss, which is observable as a rapid increase in impedance [ID: 252].

The central question is not just about coverage versus interstitial filling; it's about engineering a microstructure that creates two continuous, interpenetrating, and low-tortuosity networks (ionic and electronic) within a fixed, minimal volume, while also maintaining mechanical integrity.

### Proposed Solution: A Bimodal LPSCl Particle Size Distribution Strategy

A strategy relying on a single particle size for the LPSCl, whether for pure surface coverage or pure interstitial filling, is suboptimal. A purely interstitial approach using large LPSCl particles is a non-starter; it would leave vast regions of the NCM811 surface ionically disconnected. A purely surface-coverage approach with only fine LPSCl particles would create a dense coating but could impede electronic contact and result in poor packing density, leaving large voids that are difficult to fill [ID: 31].

The most plausible path forward is to employ a **bimodal particle size distribution for the LPSCl solid electrolyte**, which can be implemented within the mechanofusion process. This strategy has been shown to be effective for improving performance in composite cathodes [ID: 345].

Here is the proposed geometric allocation and the rationale:

1.  **Primary Component: Fine LPSCl Particles for Surface Contact.** A smaller fraction of the total LPSCl volume should consist of fine particles (let's hypothesize in the sub-micron or low-micron range). The goal of this fraction is *not* to create a perfect, hermetic coating, but to establish a sufficient density of ionic contact points across the NCM811 secondary particle surface. This ensures that a large percentage of the active material surface is ionically accessible, mitigating the primary diffusion limitations [ID: 31]. The mechanofusion process should be tuned with lower energy initially to promote the attachment of these fine particles to the NCM811 surface before densification.

2.  **Secondary Component: Coarse LPSCl Particles for Interstitial Filling and Bulk Conduction.** The majority of the LPSCl volume should consist of larger particles. These coarser particles will efficiently fill the large interstitial voids between the much larger NCM811 secondary spheres. This serves two critical functions:
    *   **High Packing Density:** Mixing particle sizes is a classic method for increasing tap density. This is essential for achieving high volumetric energy density, a key goal of solid-state batteries.
    *   **Low-Tortuosity Ionic Highways:** These larger particles will form a percolating network that acts as the main "highway" for Li-ion transport through the bulk of the thick electrode, connecting the separator to the distributed fine LPSCl particles at the NCM811 surface. This is critical for reducing the overall ionic resistance of the thick electrode structure [ID: 5, 11].

### Justification and Required Experimental Verification

This bimodal strategy directly balances the competing requirements:
*   **Ionic Continuity:** Maintained by the combination of fine particles for local access and coarse particles for bulk transport [ID: 345].
*   **Electronic Accessibility:** By avoiding a complete, thick coating of LPSCl, we preserve sufficient surface area on the NCM811 for contact with the conductive additive, maintaining the electronic network. The instability of the carbon/sulfide interface makes minimizing unnecessary contact a priority [ID: 20].
*   **Dense Packing:** The bimodal distribution inherently increases the packing density of the composite cathode, which is crucial for volumetric energy density.

To validate this hypothesis, a rigorous experimental plan is necessary. I would demand the following:

*   **Systematic Variation:** Fabricate cathodes with varying fine-to-coarse LPSCl weight ratios (e.g., 20:80, 30:70, 40:60) and compare them against monofunctional controls (100% fine and 100% coarse).
*   **Electrochemical Performance:**
    *   **Rate Capability:** Test cells at C-rates from C/10 to 2C. The bimodal structure should exhibit significantly lower polarization and higher capacity retention at higher rates.
    *   **Long-Term Cycling:** Cycle the cells for at least 500 cycles at 0.5C under a controlled, moderate stack pressure (e.g., 5-15 MPa), as excessive pressure can mask microstructural flaws [ID: 27, 252]. I need to see capacity retention above 80% and a stable Coulombic efficiency >99.8%.
*   **Impedance Spectroscopy (EIS):** Perform EIS at regular intervals during cycling. I expect the bimodal structure to show a lower and more stable charge-transfer resistance (R_ct) and ionic resistance compared to the controls, indicating a superior and more durable three-phase boundary [ID: 31, 252].
*   **Post-Mortem Analysis:** This is non-negotiable. I need to see cross-sectional FIB-SEM images of the pristine and cycled cathodes to visually confirm the microstructure. EDX mapping must be used to verify the distribution of the fine and coarse LPSCl particles relative to the NCM811 and conductive carbon [ID: 135]. We must confirm that the NCM811 secondary particles remain intact and that the electrode has not delaminated.

In conclusion, simply maximizing surface coverage is a naive approach. A hierarchical, bimodal particle size distribution for the LPSCl offers the most robust and practical pathway to balancing the ionic, electronic, and volumetric requirements of a high-loading NCM811 composite cathode. The proof, as always, will be in the electrochemical data and the post-mortem analysis.
