### Diagnosis of the Problem

The fundamental challenge you're facing is the classic percolation problem, but for two distinct and competing networks: one for ions (LPSCl) and one for electrons (conductive additive). At >85 wt% active material, the volume available for the non-active components is severely constrained.

1.  **Ionic vs. Electronic Isolation:** Your core dilemma is that strategies to improve ionic conductivity often degrade electronic conductivity, and vice-versa. A perfect, uniform coating of LPSCl on every NCM811 particle would provide excellent Li-ion access to the active material surface but would simultaneously create an insulating shell, isolating the NCM811 from the conductive carbon network [ID: 325, 421]. Conversely, prioritizing the NCM-carbon network by leaving the NCM surface bare and using LPSCl only as an interstitial filler would create a massive charge-transfer impedance at the NCM|LPSCl interface, rendering most of the active material electrochemically inaccessible [ID: 5, 42].

2.  **Chemo-Mechanical Degradation:** You are using polycrystalline NCM811, which is notoriously susceptible to intergranular cracking and pulverization from the anisotropic volume changes during high-voltage cycling [ID: 22, 152, 385]. The high pressure of mechanofusion can introduce initial stress, and poor contact points can become stress concentrators, exacerbating contact loss and degradation over time [ID: 5, 252]. Any geometric arrangement must maintain intimate, stable contact under pressure and cycling.

3.  **Tortuosity and Dead Volume:** An inefficient distribution of LPSCl creates highly tortuous ionic pathways, increasing polarization and limiting rate capability, especially in thick electrodes [ID: 157]. It also leads to poorly utilized volume and reduces the practical energy density, defeating the purpose of high-loading electrodes.

A purely surface-biased or interstitial-biased allocation of a limited SE volume is doomed to fail. The former leads to electronic isolation, the latter to ionic isolation. We need a more sophisticated geometric approach.

### Proposed Solution: A Hierarchical, Mixed-Particle-Size Allocation Strategy

My recommendation is to abandon the idea of a single-modal LPSCl particle size distribution. The most robust solution within your fixed constraints is to employ a **bimodal or multimodal distribution of LPSCl particle sizes**, controlled by careful mechanofusion, to create a hierarchical structure.

This strategy deliberately allocates the limited SE into two distinct roles:

1.  **Fine LPSCl Particles for Partial Surface Functionalization:**
    *   **What it is:** A fraction of your LPSCl should be composed of fine, nano-to-sub-micron particles.
    *   **How it works:** During mechanofusion, these fine particles will have a higher surface energy and mobility, allowing them to preferentially adhere to the surface of the larger NCM811 secondary particles. The key here is to achieve *partial, non-continuous coverage*—think of it as creating ion-conducting "patches" or "islands" on the NCM811 surface. This ensures that Li-ions have low-resistance pathways to the active material surface without forming a complete, electronically insulating shell. This establishes the necessary three-phase boundary (CAM-SE-Carbon) required for the electrochemical reaction to occur.
    *   **Justification:** The performance of composite cathodes is critically dependent on minimizing the impedance at the CAM/SE interface [ID: 5, 42, 57]. Providing direct ionic contact via these fine particles is non-negotiable.

2.  **Coarse LPSCl Particles for Interstitial Network and Densification:**
    *   **What it is:** The remaining, larger fraction of your LPSCl should consist of coarser, micron-sized particles.
    *   **How it works:** These larger particles will primarily fill the interstitial voids between the NCM811 secondary spheres. Their role is twofold:
        *   **Forming the Bulk Ionic Network:** They create continuous, low-tortuosity "highways" for Li-ions to travel deep into the thick electrode structure, feeding the finer SE particles at the CAM surfaces.
        *   **Improving Packing Density:** It is a well-established principle in ceramics and powder metallurgy that mixing particle sizes leads to higher packing density. The smaller particles fill the voids between the larger ones. Here, the coarse LPSCl particles will efficiently fill the voids between the large NCM811 spheres, maximizing the volumetric loading of the entire composite.
    *   **Justification:** Research has explicitly shown that using a mixed particle-size distribution of the solid electrolyte in the cathode composite leads to superior electrochemical performance compared to a uniform size [ID: 345]. This approach directly enhances Li-ion transport throughout the electrode thickness, which is a known bottleneck [ID: 157].

### Controlling the Process with Mechanofusion

The success of this strategy hinges on controlling the mechanofusion process. You are not aiming for complete pulverization and homogenization.

*   **Low-to-Moderate Energy Mechanofusion:** The goal is to use the process to *distribute* the fine and coarse LPSCl particles into their respective locations, not to mill them all down to a uniform nanosize. Excessive milling energy or time would destroy the bimodal distribution and simply result in the failed "surface-biased" strategy, while also potentially damaging the NCM811 particles [ID: 314].
*   **Process Optimization:** You will need to systematically vary milling time and energy to find the sweet spot that achieves good dispersion and partial surface coverage without over-milling the coarse LPSCl fraction or fracturing the NCM811. The target is a densely packed structure where large NCM spheres are surrounded by coarse LPSCl fillers, and the surfaces of the NCM spheres themselves are decorated with fine LPSCl particles, leaving enough bare surface for contact with the conductive additive.

### Risks and Further Considerations

Even with this optimized geometry, you must remain vigilant about the intrinsic challenges of your material set:

*   **Interfacial Reactivity:** This geometric solution improves transport kinetics but does not eliminate the underlying chemical and electrochemical instability between NCM811 and LPSCl [ID: 52, 415]. The formation of a space-charge layer and decomposition products at the interface will still occur, albeit at a potentially reduced rate due to better contact. While outside your primary scope, this is why the field investigates buffer coatings like LiNbO₃ or Li₃PO₄ as a complementary strategy [ID: 101, 133].
*   **Mechanical Stability:** While the hierarchical packing improves initial density, the polycrystalline NCM811 will still undergo volume changes, potentially leading to contact loss over cycling [ID: 252]. The ductile nature of sulfide electrolytes helps, but it cannot completely prevent degradation. Single-crystal NCM would be more robust, but that is outside your current design space [ID: 135, 152].

### Conclusion

To optimize your fixed-component composite cathode, I strongly advise against a monolithic approach to your solid electrolyte. A **hierarchical structure using a bimodal LPSCl particle size distribution** is the most plausible path forward. Use fine LPSCl particles to create partial ionic pathways on the NCM811 surface and coarse LPSCl particles to build a dense, low-tortuosity interstitial network. This strategy directly addresses the fundamental trade-off between ionic and electronic percolation and is supported by experimental evidence showing the superiority of mixed-particle-size composites [ID: 345]. The key will be to carefully tune your mechanofusion process to achieve this specific geometry rather than simply pulverizing the components.
