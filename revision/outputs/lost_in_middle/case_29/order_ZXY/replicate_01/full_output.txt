[The Architect (Scientist A) - Round 1]
Excellent. The lack of a standardized evaluation framework is a persistent source of frustration and a major impediment to progress in the field of aqueous zinc-ion batteries (ZIBs). We are awash in papers claiming high capacity and long cycle life for MnO₂ cathodes, but the data is often incomparable, and the reported performance is frequently an artifact of the testing conditions rather than a true property of the material itself.

### Diagnosis of the Problem

The core issue is a failure to decouple the performance of the α-MnO₂ cathode from the complex and often unstable behavior of the rest of the cell. The reported capacity and stability are a convolution of at least three major confounding factors:

1.  **The Unstable Zinc Anode:** The zinc metal anode is anything but a stable counter electrode. It suffers from dendrite growth, hydrogen evolution, and corrosion, all of which consume water, alter the electrolyte pH, and can lead to cell failure long before the cathode's intrinsic limits are reached [ID: 363, 407, 468]. A failing anode can easily be misinterpreted as a failing cathode. Strategies to stabilize the anode by controlling crystal orientation [ID: 79] or alloying [ID: 123] are promising but are not universally applied, making cross-study comparisons of cathode performance impossible.

2.  **The Ambiguous and Dynamic Reaction Mechanism:** The charge storage mechanism in MnO₂ is notoriously complex and highly sensitive to the local environment. It's not a simple Zn²⁺ intercalation process. We see evidence for H⁺ intercalation [ID: 6, 28, 89], conversion reactions leading to the precipitation of insulating zinc hydroxide sulfate (ZHS) [ID: 9, 27, 103], and Mn²⁺ dissolution/deposition [ID: 432, 97]. These pathways are heavily influenced by the electrolyte composition and, critically, by local pH evolution during cycling [ID: 27, 95, 130]. Many studies either ignore this complexity or propose a mechanism based on flimsy evidence, like *ex-situ* XRD alone, which fails to capture the dynamic nature of the interface.

3.  **Inconsistent and Poorly Reported Testing Parameters:** Results are frequently published without reporting critical parameters that dictate performance. High specific capacities are often achieved at impractically low mass loadings (<1 mg/cm²) or in flooded cells with an enormous excess of electrolyte. Claims of stability are made at low current densities or for a few hundred cycles, which is insufficient. Without standardized reporting of mass loading, electrode thickness, current density (in both A/g and mA/cm²), and electrolyte volume, the data is essentially meaningless for practical evaluation [ID: 9, 113, 115].

This lack of rigor leads to a proliferation of "hero" cells that represent optimized systems, not fundamental material properties. We cannot build a robust technology on such a foundation.

### Proposed Solution: A Rigorous, Multi-Phase Evaluation Protocol

To distinguish intrinsic α-MnO₂ behavior from system artifacts, I propose a three-phase protocol. This framework is designed to systematically deconstruct the battery, analyze each component's contribution, and build back up to a practical cell configuration under controlled conditions.

---

#### **Phase 1: Intrinsic Cathode Screening in a Decoupled System**

The objective here is to eliminate the influence of the unstable Zn anode entirely.

*   **Cell Configuration:** A three-electrode flooded cell setup.
    *   **Working Electrode (WE):** The α-MnO₂ cathode. All parameters must be explicitly reported: active material mass loading (target > 2 mg/cm²), composition (e.g., 80:10:10 active:carbon:binder), binder type (e.g., aqueous CMC, which is more practical than PVdF [ID: 3]), and electrode thickness.
    *   **Counter Electrode (CE):** A stable, high-surface-area material like activated carbon that operates in a different potential window [ID: 96, 499]. **Do not use zinc metal.**
    *   **Reference Electrode (RE):** A stable reference, such as Ag/AgCl, is mandatory to accurately track the cathode potential.
*   **Electrolyte:** Start with a standardized, mild electrolyte, such as 2 M ZnSO₄ + 0.1 M MnSO₄. The addition of MnSO₄ is critical to suppress MnO₂ dissolution by Le Chatelier's principle, allowing for a clearer assessment of structural stability [ID: 66, 142].
*   **Electrochemical Testing:**
    1.  **Cyclic Voltammetry (CV):** Sweep at various rates (e.g., 0.1 to 20 mV/s) to probe reaction kinetics and qualitatively assess the contributions of diffusion-controlled vs. capacitive processes.
    2.  **Galvanostatic Cycling (GCD):** Cycle at multiple, clearly reported current densities (e.g., 0.2, 0.5, 1, 2, 5 A/g). Cycling must be performed for a minimum of **1000 cycles** to assess long-term stability. Report capacity retention and coulombic efficiency.
    3.  **Electrochemical Impedance Spectroscopy (EIS):** Perform EIS at defined states of charge (e.g., pristine, 50% discharged, fully discharged, and after 100/500/1000 cycles) to track changes in charge transfer resistance and ion diffusion limitations.

#### **Phase 2: Mechanistic Investigation**

This phase aims to answer *why* the cathode performs as it does, moving beyond simple performance metrics.

*   **Techniques (to be used in conjunction with Phase 1 setup):**
    1.  **`In-situ`/`Operando` Characterization:** This is non-negotiable for mechanistic claims.
        *   **`In-situ` XRD:** To track crystal structure evolution *during* cycling. This will definitively show whether the α-MnO₂ tunnel structure is maintained, or if it undergoes phase transformation or conversion reactions [ID: 20, 23, 30].
        *   **`In-situ` Raman/XAS:** To probe changes in the local bonding environment and the Mn oxidation state [ID: 30, 52, 453]. This helps verify the reaction pathway (e.g., Mn⁴⁺ → Mn³⁺ → Mn²⁺).
    2.  **Electrochemical Quartz Crystal Microbalance (EQCM):** To measure mass changes at the electrode surface in real-time. This is a powerful tool for differentiating between H⁺ insertion (low mass change) and Zn²⁺ insertion (high mass change) [ID: 105, 453].
    3.  **Inductively Coupled Plasma (ICP-OES/AAS):** Analyze the electrolyte after a set number of cycles (e.g., 100 cycles) to quantify the amount of dissolved manganese. This is a direct and quantitative measure of cathode instability [ID: 131, 264].

#### **Phase 3: Standardized Full-Cell Validation**

Only materials that show promise in Phases 1 and 2 should proceed to this stage. The goal is to evaluate the cathode in a practical configuration while maintaining strict control over other variables.

*   **Cell Configuration:** Two-electrode coin cell or pouch cell.
*   **Anode:** Use a standardized, high-area, and pre-cycled zinc anode to minimize its influence as a variable in the initial stages. Report the Zn:cathode capacity ratio (N/P ratio); it should be sufficiently large (>10) to ensure the cell is cathode-limited, but this must be stated.
*   **Electrolyte:** Use the same standardized electrolyte from Phase 1. Crucially, report the electrolyte volume and calculate the electrolyte-to-capacity ratio. A "flooded cell" can mask electrolyte consumption and Mn dissolution issues.
*   **Separator:** Use a standard, well-characterized separator (e.g., glass fiber). For diagnostic purposes, ion-selective membranes can be used to explicitly block Mn²⁺ or zincate crossover to confirm their role in capacity fade [ID: 135, 168].
*   **Electrochemical Testing:**
    1.  **Long-Term Cycling:** Perform GCD for at least **1000 cycles** at a practical rate (e.g., ≥1C). The cathode mass loading must be commercially relevant (target > 5 mg/cm²).
    2.  **Performance Metrics:** Report specific capacity (mAh/g), areal capacity (mAh/cm²), and energy/power densities (Wh/kg, W/kg) calculated based on the **total mass of both electrodes**, not just the active material.
    3.  **Post-Mortem Analysis:** After cycling, disassemble the cell and analyze *both* the cathode and the anode using techniques like SEM, XRD, and XPS to identify the ultimate failure mode (e.g., cathode structural collapse, Mn dissolution, anode passivation, dendrite shorting).

By implementing this rigorous, multi-phase protocol, we can begin to build a reliable, comparable dataset on the intrinsic properties of α-MnO₂. This will allow us to separate true material improvements from artifacts of the testing setup, and finally start making real, verifiable progress toward practical aqueous zinc-ion batteries.

1.  **The "Decoupled" System Isn't Truly Decoupled:** Your Phase 1 uses a three-electrode cell with an activated carbon counter electrode in a ZnSO₄ electrolyte. While this removes the Zn metal anode, it creates a zinc-ion capacitor (ZIC) system [ID: 5, 76, 273]. The cathode's behavior is still fundamentally tied to the presence and concentration of Zn²⁺ ions and the specific electrolyte chemistry. This setup doesn't allow us to establish a true baseline for the cathode's interaction with protons, which we know is a critical reaction pathway [ID: 35, 137, 267]. It conflates the material's response to H⁺ and Zn²⁺ from the very beginning.

2.  **The Anode Is a Variable, Not a Constant:** Your proposal to use a "standardized, pre-cycled zinc anode" in Phase 3 is a noble goal, but it sidesteps the central issue: the anode is the single greatest point of failure in most ZIBs. A cathode that appears stable against your "standard" anode might fail catastrophically due to pH shifts or Mn²⁺ poisoning when paired with a simple zinc foil. Conversely, a cathode that appears to fail early might actually be perfectly stable, with the failure originating entirely from the anode side. We cannot simply standardize the anode away; we must test the cathode's resilience against *both* a baseline, problematic anode and a state-of-the-art, stabilized anode. This is the only way to determine if a new cathode material is truly an improvement or if its performance is merely propped up by an idealized counter-electrode.

3.  **Inefficient Workflow:** Placing extensive, costly `in-situ` analysis (Phase 2) before practical full-cell testing (Phase 3) is a resource-intensive and slow workflow. A material might exhibit fascinating `in-situ` structural transformations but fail immediately in a pouch cell due to poor conductivity at practical mass loadings or unforeseen reactions with the binder. From an engineering standpoint, we need to fail fast. A rapid screen for practical viability should precede deep mechanistic investigation.

### A More Pragmatic, Engineering-Driven Protocol

I propose a more cyclical and system-focused protocol designed to rapidly identify and validate practical cathode materials.

---

#### **Phase A: Baseline and Best-Case Full-Cell Screening**

The objective is to quickly assess a cathode's potential in realistic, albeit simplified, full-cell configurations.

*   **Cell Configuration:** Two-electrode 2032-type coin cells.
*   **Cathode:** The α-MnO₂ material, with a practical mass loading of **3–5 mg/cm²**.
*   **Anode (Dual-Testing is Mandatory):**
    1.  **Baseline Test:** Pair the cathode with a standard zinc foil anode.
    2.  **Best-Case Test:** Pair the cathode with a state-of-the-art stabilized anode. This could be a 3D host like carbon nanofiber [ID: 296, 42] or an anode with a protective artificial SEI layer [ID: 10, 32, 65, 110, 183].
*   **Electrolyte:** A standard 2 M ZnSO₄ + 0.1 M MnSO₄ solution. The electrolyte-to-capacity ratio must be reported and kept low (e.g., <20 µL/mAh) to avoid "flooded cell" artifacts that mask dissolution and side reactions.
*   **Electrochemical Testing:**
    *   **GCD Cycling:** Cycle at a practical rate (e.g., 1C, based on theoretical capacity) for a minimum of **500 cycles**.
    *   **Primary Metric:** Coulombic efficiency (CE) and capacity retention. A cathode is only promising if it shows significantly better stability against the *stabilized anode* compared to the *baseline anode*. If it performs poorly in both, it's likely an intrinsically unstable material. If it performs well only with the stabilized anode, it tells us the material is sensitive to anode-induced failure modes.

#### **Phase B: Mechanistic Diagnosis and Failure Analysis**

Materials that show promise in Phase A (i.e., good stability against a stabilized anode) are advanced to this stage. The goal is to understand the "why" and diagnose the failure modes observed in Phase A.

*   **Three-Electrode Analysis:** Now we use the three-electrode cell with a stable reference (Ag/AgCl) to deconvolve the potentials. We test in two electrolytes:
    1.  A Zn²⁺-free acidic electrolyte to isolate the H⁺ insertion mechanism [ID: 35, 214].
    2.  The standard ZnSO₄/MnSO₄ electrolyte to study the combined H⁺/Zn²⁺ mechanism.
*   **`In-situ` and `Ex-situ` Characterization:**
    *   **`In-situ` XRD/Raman:** Use these techniques on the promising cells from Phase A to track the structural evolution and identify the true reaction pathway [ID: 30, 43, 97, 214]. Is it H⁺/Zn²⁺ co-insertion, or is there an irreversible phase change to something like ZnMn₂O₄ [ID: 492]?
    *   **ICP-OES/AAS:** Analyze the electrolyte from the failed baseline cells in Phase A to quantify Mn dissolution. This tells us if the cathode is dissolving or if the failure was purely anode-related [ID: 131, 200, 252].
    *   **Post-Mortem SEM/XPS:** Disassemble the cells from Phase A and analyze both the cathode and the anode. Look for ZHS passivation on the cathode [ID: 35], dendrites and corrosion on the anode [ID: 8, 55], and structural degradation of the cathode material.

#### **Phase C: Practical System Integration and Scalability**

The most robust and well-understood materials from Phase B are validated for real-world application.

*   **Cell Format:** Move to pouch cells [ID: 67, 139, 144]. This tests for gassing issues, electrode uniformity over a larger area, and performance under mechanical stress.
*   **Anode and N/P Ratio:** Use a stabilized anode with a lean zinc supply, targeting a low N/P ratio (e.g., < 5). This is the true test of a reversible system where both electrodes must perform efficiently [ID: 249].
*   **Electrolyte Volume:** Use a starved electrolyte condition (e.g., <10 µL/mAh) to reflect practical device constraints.
*   **Long-Term, High-Rate Cycling:** Cycle for **>1000 cycles** at a high rate (e.g., >5C). Report areal capacity (mAh/cm²) and energy density based on the total mass of the packaged cell.
*   **Scalability Assessment:** Evaluate the synthesis method for the cathode material. Is it a simple, scalable process like a one-step hydrothermal reaction [ID: 30, 284] or a complex, multi-step process with low yield? A material is only useful if it can be produced economically at scale.

This iterative protocol—Screen, Diagnose, Validate—ensures that we focus our most intensive analytical efforts on materials that have already demonstrated practical potential. It treats the entire battery as a system, acknowledging that the anode and cathode are inextricably linked, and forces us to confront the real-world challenges of mass loading, electrolyte volume, and scalability from the very beginning. This is how we move from lab curiosities to a viable energy storage technology.

If that cell fails after 50 cycles, what have you learned? Nothing definitive. A material that fails with both anodes is discarded, but it might have been a perfectly stable material simply intolerant to the specific electrolyte chemistry you've locked in. The galvanostatic charge-discharge (GCD) curve of MnO₂ is notoriously deceptive. A beautiful, flat plateau might represent reversible ion intercalation, or it could represent a highly irreversible conversion reaction that precipitates insulating phases like zinc hydroxide sulfate (ZHS) [ID: 11, 27]. Without knowing the charge storage mechanism from the outset, your 500-cycle data is uninterpretable. You could spend months cycling a material that is simply dissolving and re-precipitating, a dead end for a rechargeable battery [ID: 432]. 3.  **The Anode Problem Cannot Be Solved by the Cathode Protocol:** While I agree the anode is a critical variable, designing a protocol around it to evaluate the *cathode* is misguided. Its tolerance to Mn²⁺ poisoning or pH shifts caused by the anode is a *system integration* problem, which should be addressed in the final validation stage, not the initial screening.

### The Scientist's Refined, Mechanism-First Protocol

I propose a revised protocol that incorporates your valid concerns about practical metrics while retaining the essential, mechanism-first approach. This framework is designed to build knowledge systematically, ensuring that by the time a material reaches full-cell testing, we have high confidence in its intrinsic properties and charge storage mechanism.

---

#### **Phase 1: Intrinsic Electrochemical Signature & Proton Response**

The goal is to establish the absolute baseline behavior of the α-MnO₂ material in the absence of Zn²⁺, isolating its response to proton-mediated chemistry, which we know is a dominant pathway [ID: 6, 28, 55, 89].

*   **Cell:** Three-electrode cell with a stable reference (Ag/AgCl).
*   **Cathode:** α-MnO₂ with a practical mass loading of **3-5 mg/cm²**. This addresses your valid point on loading.
*   **Electrolyte:** A **Zn²⁺-free** mild electrolyte, such as 1 M (NH₄)₂SO₄ [ID: 5, 34, 120] or MgSO₄ [ID: 13]. This isolates the proton/cation (NH₄⁺, Mg²⁺) intercalation behavior without confounding variables from Zn²⁺.
*   **Testing:**
    *   Cyclic Voltammetry (CV) to identify the fundamental redox potentials.
    *   GCD cycling for 100 cycles at C/5.
    *   **Crucial Step:** `in-situ` Raman or XRD for the first few cycles to confirm a simple intercalation mechanism versus a destructive phase change. This is a quick check to avoid wasting time on fundamentally unstable materials.
*   **Go/No-Go Metric:** Does the material exhibit reversible cycling without significant structural degradation in a simple, non-Zn²⁺ system? If not, it's unlikely to survive the more complex zinc environment.

#### **Phase 2: Unveiling the Zinc-Interaction Mechanism**

Now, we introduce zinc ions to understand how they alter the established baseline mechanism. Materials must pass Phase 1.

*   **Cell:** Same three-electrode setup.
*   **Electrolyte:** Standard 2 M ZnSO₄ + 0.1 M MnSO₄.
*   **Testing:**
    *   **Definitive `in-situ` Analysis:** This is non-negotiable and must be done here. `in-situ` XRD and XAS are required to track phase evolution. Are we seeing H⁺/Zn²⁺ co-insertion into the tunnels [ID: 20]? Or are we seeing the formation of ZHS byproducts, indicating a conversion-extrusion mechanism [ID: 11, 27]? Is the α-MnO₂ structure stable or does it transform into a layered or spinel phase [ID: 99, 108]?
    *   **Kinetic Analysis:** Use GITT and EIS to quantify and compare the diffusion coefficients of H⁺ (from Phase 1) and Zn²⁺ (in this phase). This will tell us if Zn²⁺ transport is kinetically sluggish, as is often the case [ID: 8, 94].
*   **Go/No-Go Metric:** Does the material maintain its structural integrity and demonstrate a reversible H⁺/Zn²⁺ co-insertion mechanism? Materials that undergo irreversible phase changes or show dominant conversion reactions are rejected.

#### **Phase 3: Full-Cell Performance and Failure Mode Analysis**

Only materials with a known, stable mechanism from Phase 2 are advanced. Here, we stress-test the cathode in a realistic system, incorporating your excellent suggestion of dual-anode testing.

*   **Cell:** Two-electrode coin cells.
*   **Anode:**
    1.  **Baseline:** Standard zinc foil.
    2.  **Stabilized:** Anode with a protective coating or modified structure [ID: 62, 79, 291].
*   **Cycling:** Long-term cycling (**>1000 cycles**) at a practical rate (e.g., 1C). Monitor capacity, CE, and voltage profiles.
*   **Post-Mortem Diagnostics:**
    *   **ICP-OES:** Analyze the electrolyte from both cell types after failure. Did the cathode dissolve significantly [ID: 264]? This quantifies the material's chemical stability.
    *   **SEM/XPS:** Analyze both electrodes. Compare the cathode from the baseline cell to the one from the stabilized cell. Is the degradation morphology different? This directly links anode behavior to cathode stability.
*   **Go/No-Go Metric:** The material must show excellent stability (>80% retention over 1000 cycles) when paired with the *stabilized* anode. Its performance with the baseline anode provides crucial data on its sensitivity to anode-induced failure modes.

#### **Phase 4: Scalability and Practical Demonstration**

This phase mirrors your "Phase C" and is the final gate before considering a material truly promising.

*   **Cell Format:** Pouch cells to test for gassing, uniformity, and mechanical integrity [ID: 1, 6].
*   **N/P Ratio & Electrolyte:** Use a lean anode (low N/P ratio, e.g., <4) and starved electrolyte conditions (<10 µL/mAh) to simulate a real device [ID: 9].
*   **Synthesis Scalability:** Evaluate the synthesis route. A simple, one-step hydrothermal [ID: 102] or co-precipitation method [ID: 18] is far more viable than a complex, multi-step process with low yields.

This refined protocol is not linear; it is a funnel. It uses fundamental, mechanism-driven analysis to efficiently filter out unsuitable candidates early, ensuring that the most resource-intensive cycling and validation efforts are spent only on materials with a proven, stable foundation. This is not slower; it is smarter. It prevents us from chasing electrochemical "fool's gold" and builds the knowledge base needed to rationally design the next generation of materials, moving our field beyond empirical screening and toward predictable science.

This method aims to avoid conflating failure modes, such as cathode structural collapse, dissolution, surface passivation, and anode-driven failure [Scientist A: ID: 2, 27, 79]. The core principle is that understanding the fundamental reaction pathway via `in-situ` analysis early [Scientist A: ID: 23, 28] is the most efficient path to rational material design.

*   **Scientist B (The Engineer)** argued for a pragmatic, viability-first approach focused on failing fast in a relevant environment. This protocol begins by immediately screening cathodes in a practical two-electrode coin cell against standard zinc foil [Scientist B: ID: 355]. The key insight is to use simple electrochemical markers like Coulombic Efficiency (CE) as a non-invasive diagnostic for anode health [Scientist B: ID: 6, 49], allowing for a rapid assessment of whether failure is anode- or cathode-driven. This approach reserves resource-intensive characterization for materials that have already demonstrated a baseline of performance in the target system [Scientist B: ID: 489], avoiding wasted effort on materials that are academically interesting but practically non-viable [Scientist B: ID: 258].

The primary point of contention is the initial screening step. Scientist A argues that Scientist B's approach is a "black box" that conflates failure modes, while Scientist B contends that Scientist A's initial test in a Zn²⁺-free environment is irrelevant and risks generating "false positives" that are not viable in a real ZIB.

### 2. Director's Assessment and Recommended Path Forward

Both positions have significant merit. Scientist A is correct that a simple two-electrode test can be diagnostically ambiguous. Scientist B is correct that testing in a non-target electrolyte is inefficient and potentially misleading. A successful protocol must be both diagnostically powerful and practically relevant from the outset.

Therefore, we will adopt a hybrid protocol that merges the diagnostic rigor of the Architect's approach with the pragmatic, systems-level focus of the Engineer's. This "diagnostics-in-situ" framework is designed to provide maximum insight with minimum deviation from a practical testbed.

**The Recommended Hybrid Protocol:**

1.  **Phase 1: Rapid Diagnostic Screening:**
    *   **Configuration:** A **three-electrode coin cell** setup. This incorporates a stable reference electrode (e.g., Ag/AgCl, or a simple Zn wire reference) into a scalable, practical format.
    *   **Components:** The α-MnO₂ working electrode at a practical mass loading (≥3 mg/cm²) [Scientist B: ID: 355], a standard zinc foil counter electrode, and a standard glass fiber separator.
    *   **Electrolyte:** The target environment: **2 M ZnSO₄ + 0.1 M MnSO₄**. The MnSO₄ additive is a pragmatic step to suppress a known and immediate dissolution failure mode, allowing other potential issues to be observed [Scientist A: ID: 66, 142], [Scientist B: ID: 283, 325].
    *   **Testing:** Short-term galvanostatic cycling (100 cycles at ~1 A g⁻¹).
    *   **Go/No-Go Analysis:** During this single test, we will simultaneously monitor:
        *   **Anode Health:** Average Coulombic Efficiency (<99.5% indicates dominant anode-side reactions) [Scientist B: ID: 49].
        *   **Cathode Stability:** The potential profile of the α-MnO₂ cathode vs. the reference. Rapid capacity fade while CE remains high points to an intrinsic cathode issue [Scientist B: ID: 350]. A significant drop in the discharge voltage plateau suggests irreversible phase transformation [Scientist B: ID: 492].
        *   **Cell Health:** The voltage profiles of the full cell and both individual electrodes. This immediately decouples failure modes without resorting to an irrelevant test environment.

2.  **Phase 2: Targeted Failure Analysis:**
    *   Materials that pass Phase 1 (i.e., show stable cathode potential and high CE) proceed directly to Phase 3.
    *   For failed cells, the Phase 1 data dictates the next step. If the cathode potential was unstable, we will perform targeted post-mortem analysis: **ICP-OES** on the electrolyte to quantify Mn dissolution [Scientist A: ID: 264], [Scientist B: ID: 264] and **ex-situ XRD/SEM** on the cathode to identify phase changes or ZHS passivation [Scientist A: ID: 27], [Scientist B: ID: 15].
    *   Only after a material demonstrates promise in Phase 1 will we allocate resources for definitive **`in-situ` XRD/Raman** to confirm the charge storage mechanism of a *known-good* candidate [Scientist B: ID: 489].

3.  **Phase 3: System Integration & Long-Term Validation:**
    *   Promising cathodes will be tested in two-electrode coin cells for long-term cycling (>1000 cycles). Crucially, this will be a comparative test: one cell against standard Zn foil and one against a **stabilized anode** (e.g., surface-coated, 3D host) [Scientist A: ID: 79, 123], [Scientist B: ID: 10, 42, 65]. This directly quantifies the cathode's resilience to anode-induced failure.

4.  **Phase 4: Practical Application & Scalability:**
    *   The top-performing systems from Phase 3 will be scaled up to **pouch cells**. Here, we will impose real-world constraints: lean electrolyte, low N/P ratios (<4) [Scientist A: ID: 9], [Scientist B: ID: 355], and monitoring for gas evolution (swelling) [Scientist B: ID: 21, 345]. The scalability and cost of the cathode synthesis route will also be formally evaluated [Scientist A: ID: 336, 415].

This unified protocol leverages our resources efficiently. It avoids the academic detour of a Zn²⁺-free test while providing far greater diagnostic clarity than a simple two-electrode screen. It ensures that we only invest heavily in materials that have proven their viability in a relevant environment, providing the most direct path to a commercially viable aqueous ZIB.
