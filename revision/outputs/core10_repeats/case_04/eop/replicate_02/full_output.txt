[EOP Answer]
The challenge you've outlined is a classic materials architecture problem. The performance of your V2O3/CNT cathode is entirely dependent on how you structure the composite at the nano- and microscale. The inherent issues with vanadium oxides in aqueous ZIBs—namely, sluggish kinetics due to strong Zn2+ interactions and structural degradation from volume changes—cannot be solved by simply mixing the components [ID: 3, ID: 24]. A rationally designed, synergistic architecture is required.

The core failure mode you are facing is the breakdown of the electrode's integrity and transport pathways. Simply anchoring V2O3 particles onto the surface of a CNT microsphere is insufficient; the V2O3 will detach during cycling due to mechanical stress, leading to a loss of both electrical contact and active material.

The solution is to construct a **hierarchically porous composite microsphere** where V2O3 is not just anchored *on*, but intimately integrated *within* a 3D conductive CNT network. This architecture directly addresses the coupled problems of ion transport, electronic continuity, and structural stability.

Here is the architectural design and the mechanistic rationale:

**Proposed Architecture: V2O3 Nanocrystal-Interwoven CNT Microspheres with Sacrificial Pore Templating**

The ideal structure is a microsphere composed of a 3D web of carbon nanotubes, with V2O3 nanocrystals or even an amorphous V2O3 phase grown *in-situ* and interwoven within this web. This structure should possess both macrovoids for bulk electrolyte ingress and mesopores within the V2O3/CNT composite walls for intimate electrolyte contact.

**Synergistic Mechanism:**

1.  **Accelerated Zn2+ Transport:** The hierarchical porosity is critical. The large macrovoids, created by a templating method, act as ion reservoirs, allowing the aqueous electrolyte to rapidly penetrate the core of the microsphere. From there, the mesoporous nature of the V2O3/CNT composite walls provides abundant contact with the electrolyte and shortens the Zn2+ diffusion path to the active material surface [ID: 1, ID: 12]. Furthermore, using an amorphous V2O3 phase can provide more isotropic Zn2+ diffusion routes, further accelerating kinetics compared to a crystalline counterpart [ID: 59, ID: 179].

2.  **Preserved Electrical Continuity:** The interconnected 3D CNT network forms a continuous, highly conductive scaffold throughout the entire microsphere. This ensures every V2O3 nanocrystal maintains excellent electronic contact, which is essential for overcoming the inherently poor conductivity of vanadium oxide and achieving high-rate capability [ID: 174, ID: 138]. This robust network is far superior to simple point-contacts from physical mixing.

3.  **Enhanced Structural Stability:** By embedding the V2O3 nanocrystals within the flexible and mechanically robust CNT matrix, the strain from volume expansion and contraction during Zn2+ intercalation/de-intercalation is effectively buffered and accommodated [ID: 1, ID: 174]. The CNTs act as a structural backbone, preventing the pulverization of V2O3 particles and their detachment from the conductive network, which is the primary cause of capacity fade in such systems. This design also makes the electrode binder-free, which improves overall energy density [ID: 78].

**Addressing Your Process Concern with Spray Drying:**

Your concern about spray drying is valid, as a poorly controlled process can lead to a fragile, low-density product or blocked pores. However, evidence shows that a one-pot aerosol process or spray pyrolysis is a highly effective and scalable method to create the exact architecture I am proposing [ID: 40, ID: 554].

To achieve the desired structure, you should prepare a colloidal spray solution containing a vanadium precursor (e.g., ammonium vanadate), well-dispersed CNTs, and a sacrificial templating agent, such as polystyrene (PS) nanobeads [ID: 513]. During spray drying, these components will self-assemble into composite microspheres. A subsequent calcination (post-treatment) step is crucial. This step serves two purposes: it converts the vanadium precursor into the desired V2O3 phase and it burns away the PS beads, leaving behind the well-defined macrovoids needed for electrolyte penetration [ID: 40, ID: 513]. This approach ensures uniform distribution of the V2O3 and CNTs while simultaneously engineering the required porosity [ID: 508, ID 554].

For further performance enhancement, the V2O3 itself can be engineered. Creating oxygen vacancies [ID: 2, ID: 16] or pre-intercalating the structure with stabilizing ions like Na+ or Ca2+ can expand the V-O layers, which has been shown to stabilize the structure and facilitate faster ion diffusion [ID: 6, ID: 7]. For instance, incorporating a conductive polymer like polyaniline (PANI) can expand the interlayer spacing of the vanadium oxide, effectively shielding the electrostatic interactions with Zn2+ and dramatically boosting the Zn2+ diffusion coefficient [ID: 274, ID: 116, ID: 92].

In summary, the key is to move beyond simple surface anchoring and fabricate an integrated, hierarchical composite. By co-assembling V2O3 and CNTs into porous microspheres using a templated spray-drying process, you create a structure that provides short diffusion paths for ions, continuous pathways for electrons, and mechanical reinforcement to withstand cycling stresses. This is how you solve the transport, stability, and continuity bottlenecks simultaneously.
