[EOP Answer]
You've hit on the central challenge in evaluating MnO₂ cathodes for aqueous zinc-ion batteries. The literature is filled with performance data that is difficult to compare because the observed capacity and stability are often dictated by system-level artifacts rather than the intrinsic properties of the manganese dioxide itself. The issues you've outlined—manganese dissolution, pH shifts leading to parasitic products like zinc hydroxide sulfate (ZHS), and an unstable zinc anode—are precisely the variables that obscure the true behavior of the cathode [ID: 2, 4, 27, 508]. A material is only as good as the problem it solves, but to know if it's solved, we first need a reliable test.

To distinguish the intrinsic electrochemical behavior of α-MnO₂ from these confounding factors, we need a rigorous, standardized evaluation framework. This is not about inventing a new material, but about creating a controlled environment to fairly judge the one we have. Here is the testing protocol and evaluation framework I would propose.

### Stage 1: Standardized Cell Assembly to Isolate the Cathode

The primary goal here is to minimize variability from the counter electrode, electrolyte, and physical cell construction. This creates a stable baseline against which the α-MnO₂ performance can be measured.

1.  **Three-Electrode Configuration:** This is non-negotiable for isolating cathode behavior. Standard two-electrode coin cells conflate the potentials and impedances of both electrodes. Use a three-electrode setup (e.g., a Swagelok-type cell or beaker cell) with the α-MnO₂ as the working electrode, a large-surface-area, polished zinc foil as the counter electrode, and a stable reference electrode (e.g., Ag/AgCl). This allows for precise measurement of the cathode's potential, independent of the zinc anode's fluctuations.

2.  **Electrolyte Formulation:** The electrolyte is a major source of artifacts.
    *   **Base Electrolyte:** Use a standard 2 M ZnSO₄ aqueous solution. This is the most common electrolyte and provides a good starting point for comparability.
    *   **Suppressing Mn Dissolution:** The most significant artifact is the dissolution of MnO₂. This is often driven by the disproportionation of Mn³⁺ into Mn²⁺ and Mn⁴⁺, which pollutes the electrolyte and leads to irreversible capacity loss [ID: 179]. To suppress this, the electrolyte must be saturated with manganese ions. I recommend adding 0.1 M to 0.3 M MnSO₄ to the 2 M ZnSO₄ electrolyte. This common-ion effect significantly inhibits the dissolution of MnO₂ from the cathode, allowing us to study its structural response to ion insertion rather than its dissolution-deposition chemistry [ID: 116, 142, 538].
    *   **Electrolyte Volume:** Use a flooded cell configuration with a large, defined volume of electrolyte. This minimizes drastic changes in the bulk electrolyte pH and concentration, which are known to cause precipitation of species like ZHS [ID: 27]. While local pH at the electrode surface will still change, this approach prevents the entire system from drifting uncontrollably.

3.  **Standardized Electrode Preparation:**
    *   **Cathode Formulation:** Prepare electrodes with a consistent, reported composition, for instance, 70% α-MnO₂, 20% conductive carbon (e.g., Super P), and 10% binder. Using a water-soluble binder like sodium carboxymethyl cellulose (CMC) is preferable to PVdF, as it avoids harsh solvents and has been shown to improve performance and homogeneity [ID: 3, 346].
    *   **Mass Loading:** This is a critical parameter. For probing intrinsic material properties, test at a low, standardized mass loading (1–2 mg cm⁻²). This minimizes ion diffusion limitations within the electrode. However, to assess practical viability, a separate set of tests at a higher, more application-relevant mass loading (>5 mg cm⁻²) is necessary to reveal transport bottlenecks [ID: 9, 850]. All reported capacities must specify the mass loading.

### Stage 2: Rigorous Electrochemical Testing Protocol

With a standardized cell, we can now execute a testing sequence designed to probe specific properties and failure modes.

1.  **Initial Characterization:**
    *   **Cyclic Voltammetry (CV):** Perform CV scans at various rates (e.g., 0.1 to 10 mV s⁻¹). This helps distinguish between diffusion-controlled intercalation processes and faster, surface-capacitive contributions to charge storage [ID: 28, 67].
    *   **Electrochemical Impedance Spectroscopy (EIS):** Measure EIS at open-circuit voltage before cycling. This provides a baseline for the cell's charge-transfer resistance (Rct) and solution resistance (Rs) [ID: 8].
    *   **Galvanostatic Intermittent Titration Technique (GITT):** Perform a GITT measurement on the first cycle to determine the chemical diffusion coefficient of the charge carrier (Zn²⁺ and/or H⁺) within the α-MnO₂ structure [ID: 14, 20]. This provides a quantitative measure of the cathode's intrinsic kinetics.

2.  **Cycling Performance Evaluation:**
    *   **Rate Capability:** Cycle the cell at various C-rates (e.g., from 0.1C to 10C, where 1C = 308 mA g⁻¹ for a one-electron process). This tests the material's ability to sustain capacity at high power demands.
    *   **Long-Term Cyclability:** Cycle the cell at a moderate-to-high rate (e.g., 2C or 5C) for at least 1000 cycles. Track specific capacity, coulombic efficiency, and the evolution of the charge-discharge voltage profiles. Degradation in the voltage profile can indicate an increase in polarization or a change in the reaction mechanism [ID: 286].
    *   **Voltage Window Control:** It is crucial to limit the discharge depth. Deep discharge can trigger irreversible conversion reactions and structural collapse [ID: 1, 286]. A cutoff voltage around 1.0 V vs. Zn/Zn²⁺ is often necessary to stay within the more reversible H⁺/Zn²⁺ intercalation regime.

### Stage 3: Post-Mortem and Mechanistic Analysis

Electrochemical data alone is insufficient. We must correlate performance with physical and chemical changes in the electrode.

1.  **Mandatory Post-Cycling Characterization:** Harvest the α-MnO₂ cathodes after a defined number of cycles (e.g., 1, 10, 100, and end-of-life).
    *   **XRD Analysis:** Use X-ray diffraction to track changes in the crystal structure. This will reveal if the α-MnO₂ tunnel structure is stable or if it undergoes transformation to other phases like layered zinc-birnessite, spinel (ZnMn₂O₄), or if byproducts like ZHS have deposited on the surface [ID: 11, 20, 99].
    *   **Microscopy (SEM/TEM):** Use scanning and transmission electron microscopy to observe changes in particle morphology, cracking, or the growth of surface layers [ID: 84, 526].
    *   **Spectroscopy (XPS):** X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy is essential for analyzing the surface chemistry, particularly the valence state of manganese (e.g., Mn⁴⁺, Mn³⁺, Mn²⁺) and the elemental composition of any surface films [ID: 20, 23].

2.  **Quantitative Dissolution Analysis:** After cycling, analyze the electrolyte using Inductively Coupled Plasma (ICP-OES or ICP-MS) to quantify the concentration of dissolved manganese [ID: 237, 399]. This provides a direct measure of cathode dissolution, allowing you to correlate it with capacity fade and determine the effectiveness of the MnSO₄ additive.

By implementing this three-stage framework, we can systematically control for the most common artifacts in ZIB testing. Stabilizing the anode and electrolyte allows the electrochemical tests to primarily reflect the cathode's response. The multi-tiered testing protocol (low vs. high mass loading, varied C-rates) helps differentiate intrinsic material properties from engineering limitations. Finally, rigorous post-mortem analysis links observed performance changes directly to structural and chemical degradation pathways within the α-MnO₂ material itself. This approach moves us from ambiguous, system-dependent results toward a reliable evaluation of whether a given α-MnO₂ cathode is truly viable for a practical aqueous zinc-ion battery.
