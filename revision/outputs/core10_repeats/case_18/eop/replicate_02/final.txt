Based on your experimental situation, your approach of using a hierarchical structure—metal sulfide nanoparticles encapsulated within hollow carbon nanosphere-aggregated microspheres—is a sound strategy for tackling the challenges of potassium-ion storage. The larger size of K+ ions indeed exacerbates the well-known problems of volume expansion and sluggish kinetics associated with metal sulfide anodes [ID: 126, 499]. The key to success lies in the precise engineering of this architecture to provide both mechanical stability and efficient transport pathways.

Here is my analysis of how to optimize your material design, focusing on your specific bottlenecks.

### 1. Stabilizing the Structure Against Volumetric Stress

Your primary bottleneck is accommodating the massive volume changes during potassiation/depotassiation. A simple hollow structure is a good start, but for K-ion systems, a more sophisticated design is required to maintain mechanical integrity.

The most effective strategy is to create a **multi-yolk-shell architecture** [ID: 57]. In your system, the metal sulfide nanoparticles are the "yolks" and the individual hollow carbon nanospheres are the "shells". This design is superior to a large, single void because it distributes stress across multiple, smaller units. A recent study on Fe₁-ₓS@C multi-yolk-shell structures for potassium storage demonstrated that having tunable interior void space is critical for achieving ultra-stable cycling over thousands of cycles [ID: 57]. The void inside each hollow carbon nanosphere must be large enough to accommodate the full expansion of the encapsulated sulfide nanoparticles without exerting excessive stress on the carbon shell. This prevents the pulverization of the entire microsphere and maintains electrical contact.

The synthesis must be carefully controlled to achieve this. A partial sulfidation strategy, where a metal-oxide precursor is not fully converted, can be used to create these voids in a self-templated manner, which is a clever and potentially scalable approach [ID: 57]. Alternatively, using sacrificial templates like silica or polymers during the synthesis of the hollow carbon nanospheres can also create the necessary internal space [ID: 504, 543].

### 2. Preventing Transport Bottlenecks

To avoid "choking" the reaction, you need to ensure pathways for both electrons and K-ions remain open throughout the entire microsphere.

**For K-ion transport**, you need hierarchical porosity. This means having pores at multiple length scales.
*   **Mesopores in the nanosphere shells:** The shells of the individual hollow carbon nanospheres must be porous to allow the electrolyte to penetrate and reach the encapsulated metal sulfide nanoparticles [ID: 124, 527]. Without this, the active material is isolated.
*   **Macropores between nanospheres:** The aggregation of the hollow nanospheres into a microsphere must create a network of interconnected macropores. These larger channels act as highways for the electrolyte to quickly permeate the entire secondary particle, ensuring that even the innermost nanospheres are accessible [ID: 6, 141].

Your concern about spray drying collapsing this void network is valid. One effective method to preserve this inter-sphere porosity during synthesis is to use a washable template, such as NaCl crystals, during the spray pyrolysis or drying process. These templates create defined "nanorooms" or voids between the primary particles and can be easily washed away afterward, leaving a robust and open porous structure [ID: 29].

**For electron transport**, the carbon framework is essential due to the low intrinsic conductivity of metal sulfides [ID: 1, 42]. The aggregation of carbon nanospheres must form a continuous conductive network. Heteroatom doping, particularly with nitrogen, is a proven strategy to enhance the electronic conductivity of the carbon host and create more active sites [ID: 50, 82, 111]. The use of nitrogen-containing precursors like polyaniline or dopamine during the carbon sphere synthesis is a common and effective method [ID: 514, 537].

### 3. Mitigating Polysulfide Dissolution and Maintaining Structural Integrity

The dissolution of potassium polysulfide (KₓSᵧ) intermediates is a critical failure mode that leads to capacity fade, similar to what is seen in Li-S and Na-S systems [ID: 1, 107]. Your hollow sphere design provides a primary defense through physical confinement.

To significantly improve stability, this physical confinement must be coupled with **chemical anchoring**. By doping the carbon host with heteroatoms like nitrogen or sulfur, you create polar sites on the carbon surface that have a strong chemical affinity for the polar KₓSᵧ intermediates [ID: 2, 49]. This synergistic effect of physical confinement and chemical adsorption effectively traps the polysulfides within the hollow nanospheres, preventing them from dissolving into the electrolyte and shuttling to the anode. This greatly improves Coulombic efficiency and cycle life [ID: 529]. Direct evidence for K-ion batteries shows that constructing a heterostructure with a polar sulfide like VS₄ can effectively anchor KₓSᵧ intermediates, leading to dramatically improved lifespan [ID: 49]. Similarly, encapsulating FeS₂ in a graphene shell was shown to inhibit the polysulfide shuttle effect in KIBs [ID: 348].

The carbon shell must also be mechanically robust to survive thousands of expansion/contraction cycles without fracturing. Yolk-shell structures, where the active material is not directly bonded to the shell, have shown excellent durability because the shell remains intact while the core expands and shrinks freely [ID: 104, 229]. Your design should aim for this, ensuring the metal sulfide nanoparticles are confined within, but not rigidly attached to, the inner wall of the hollow carbon spheres.

In summary, the optimal design for your material should feature:
1.  **Multi-yolk-shell architecture:** Metal sulfide nanoparticles (yolks) with sufficient void space inside each robust hollow carbon nanosphere (shell) to accommodate volume change [ID: 57].
2.  **Hierarchical porosity:** Mesoporous shells on the carbon nanospheres for local electrolyte access and macroporous channels between the aggregated spheres for bulk electrolyte percolation [ID: 6, 124].
3.  **Chemically active carbon host:** Nitrogen and/or sulfur doping of the carbon framework to enhance conductivity and chemically anchor potassium polysulfides [ID: 2, 49].
4.  **Scalable synthesis:** A process like spray drying/pyrolysis, possibly using a washable salt template, can create the desired hierarchical microspheres in a scalable manner [ID: 29].
