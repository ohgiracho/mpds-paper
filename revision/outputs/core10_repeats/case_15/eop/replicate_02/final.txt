Based on your experimental situation, the core challenge is not merely hosting selenium, but architecting a carbon framework that simultaneously manages polyselenide dissolution, facilitates rapid transport of both ions and electrons, and maintains structural integrity under high active material loading. The bottlenecks you've identified—active species loss, transport limitations, and framework continuity—are all interconnected symptoms of a fundamental materials architecture problem. A MOF-derived bimodally porous carbon nanofiber is an excellent starting point, but its success hinges on precisely engineering its hierarchical structure and surface chemistry.

The primary failure mode in any Li-Se system is the polyselenide shuttle effect, where soluble lithium polyselenides (Li₂Seₙ, n > 2) dissolve into the electrolyte, migrate to the anode, and cause capacity fade and low Coulombic efficiency [ID: 9, 86]. Simply confining selenium in pores is insufficient; we must immobilize it. Therefore, the design must be guided by the principle of creating a multifunctional host that provides both strong physical confinement and robust chemical anchoring.

Here is how we should approach the design of the MOF-derived carbon nanofiber host:

The ideal architecture is a three-dimensional, interconnected network of nitrogen-doped, hierarchically porous carbon nanofibers. This structure can be achieved by electrospinning a polymer blended with a metal-organic framework (MOF) precursor, such as a Zeolitic Imidazolate Framework (ZIF), followed by carbonization. This process creates a continuous fiber network where each fiber possesses a bimodal or even multimodal pore distribution derived from the MOF pyrolysis.

The function of each architectural element is critical:

1.  **Micropores for Confinement and Reaction Pathway Control:** The ZIF-derived carbonization process is excellent for creating a high density of micropores, typically in the sub-nanometer range (e.g., 0.4–0.55 nm) [ID: 15]. These micropores are the primary sites for selenium encapsulation via a melt-diffusion process. This severe nanoconfinement is not just a physical cage; it fundamentally alters the electrochemical reaction pathway. Instead of a multistep reduction involving soluble polyselenides, selenium confined in such small pores can undergo a direct, solid-state transformation to the final discharge product, Li₂Se [ID: 41, 38]. This effectively eliminates the formation of soluble intermediates, which is the root cause of the shuttle effect and capacity decay.

2.  **Nitrogen-Doping for Chemical Anchoring:** ZIFs are rich in nitrogen, and their pyrolysis naturally yields a nitrogen-doped carbon matrix [ID: 15, 711]. This is not a trivial feature; it is essential for performance. The nitrogen atoms create polar sites on the carbon surface. As confirmed by density functional theory calculations for the analogous sulfur system, these polar sites have a strong chemical affinity for polyselenide species [ID: 511]. This creates a "chemical anchor" that strongly binds any polyselenides that might form, preventing their dissolution into the electrolyte [ID: 9, 596]. This chemical interaction is far more effective than relying on physical confinement alone and is key to achieving long cycle life and high Coulombic efficiency, often exceeding 99% [ID: 15]. Furthermore, N-doping enhances the electronic conductivity of the carbon host, facilitating rapid charge transfer for the redox reactions [ID: 15].

3.  **Mesopores as Ion Reservoirs and Buffering Space:** The bimodal structure, with mesopores (2-50 nm) complementing the micropores, is crucial for transport and stability. These larger pores act as ion-transport channels and reservoirs for the electrolyte, ensuring that lithium ions can rapidly access the selenium stored deep within the microporous framework [ID: 15, 561]. This hierarchical porosity is essential for good rate capability. Additionally, these mesoporous voids serve as crucial buffer space to accommodate the large volumetric expansion (about 77%) that selenium undergoes during lithiation, thereby preserving the structural integrity of the nanofiber and preventing pulverization over many cycles [ID: 15, 502].

4.  **Interconnected Nanofiber Network for Electronic Percolation:** The one-dimensional nanofiber morphology, when formed into a freestanding or slurry-cast mat, creates a three-dimensional, continuous, and highly conductive network [ID: 2]. This architecture provides an uninterrupted "highway" for electrons from the current collector to every particle of active material, ensuring high selenium utilization even at high mass loadings (>60 wt%) [ID: 4, 518]. This robust framework directly addresses your concern about the host separating into poorly wired domains, as the interwoven fibers maintain excellent electrical contact throughout the electrode during cycling [ID: 9].

In summary, the solution lies in a synergistic architecture. By using a ZIF-based precursor to create N-doped carbon nanofibers, we produce a host that solves the key challenges simultaneously. The micropores provide extreme physical confinement to promote a shuttle-free, solid-state reaction pathway [ID: 41]. The inherent N-doping provides strong chemical anchoring sites for any residual polyselenides and boosts conductivity [ID: 15, 511]. The mesopores ensure rapid ion transport and accommodate volume changes. Finally, the interconnected 1D nanofiber network guarantees outstanding electronic percolation and structural stability [ID: 2]. This rationally designed, hierarchical host is the most promising path to balancing confinement, transport, and conductivity for a high-performance, high-loading Li-Se cathode.
