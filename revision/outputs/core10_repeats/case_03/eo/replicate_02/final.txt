Based on the provided evidence, the design and processing of NiMoO4 microspheres with internal nanovoids for durable, high-rate lithium-ion storage requires a careful balance of structural engineering to accommodate volume changes, incorporation of conductive pathways, and process control to achieve a practical tap density. Spray drying emerges as a key scalable manufacturing technique capable of addressing these competing requirements.

**Structural Design for Void Buffering and Ion Transport**

To mitigate the structural damage and progressive capacity fade caused by the large volume changes inherent to conversion-type anodes, an architecture with internal voids is necessary. The evidence suggests several viable designs. Microspheres containing internal "empty nanovoids" have been successfully prepared for related multicomponent (Mo, Ni) metal sulfide and selenide systems, demonstrating a promising structural template [ID: 431]. This type of architecture provides internal space to buffer volume expansion, which is critical for maintaining mechanical coherence during cycling. Other analogous void-containing structures produced for high-volume-change anodes include yolk-shell microspheres, which feature a void between the active core and an outer shell [ID: 437], and hollow nanospheres, which can be formed through a Kirkendall diffusion mechanism during post-synthesis heat treatment [ID: 461].

These hierarchical structures, such as core-shell heterostructures, are suggested to provide good strain accommodation [ID: 27]. The internal porosity created by the assembly of primary nanoparticles into a microsphere is also crucial for ensuring fast diffusion of the liquid electrolyte throughout the electrode material [ID: 217]. However, excessive or poorly connected porosity can lower tap density and create tortuous transport paths. Therefore, the ideal structure would consist of microspheres assembled from primary NiMoO4 nanocrystals, with engineered voids between them that are large enough to buffer volume changes but small enough to maintain interconnected pathways for ion and electron transport.

**Processing via Spray Drying**

Spray drying is a versatile and scalable method for producing the desired microsphere morphology from a precursor slurry [ID: 431, 353, 424]. The process allows for the assembly of nanoscale building blocks, such as hydrothermally prepared nanowires or co-precipitated nanoparticles, into porous microspheres [ID: 499, 511]. The final morphology—whether solid, porous, or hollow—can be controlled by both the process parameters and the slurry composition. For instance, the choice between airflow and centrifugal atomization can determine whether hollow or solid spheres are formed [ID: 417]. Furthermore, adding sacrificial templates or pore-forming agents to the precursor slurry, such as polymers, sucrose, or ammonium salts, is an effective strategy to create internal porosity upon their removal during subsequent calcination [ID: 644, 451]. A post-synthesis heat treatment is typically necessary to crystallize the NiMoO4 phase, remove binders or templates, and control the final porosity and surface area [ID: 360, 431].

**Balancing Conductivity and Tap Density**

A key trade-off in designing porous architectures is the potential reduction in electronic conductivity and tap density. To address conductivity, a carbon network should be integrated into the microsphere structure. This can be achieved by co-processing a carbon source within the spray-drying slurry. Examples include using sucrose as a binder that carbonizes during heat treatment to coat the primary particles [ID: 451, 524], or incorporating conductive additives like carbon black or graphene sheets directly into the precursor slurry to form a three-dimensional conductive framework [ID: 420, 451]. A "double carbon coating" process, where primary nanocrystals are first coated and then assembled into a larger carbon-coated microsphere via spray drying, has been shown to be effective for other electrode materials [ID: 353]. This integrated carbon network not only enhances electronic conductivity but also helps buffer volume changes and prevent the aggregation of primary nanoparticles [ID: 451, 554].

While creating voids can lower volumetric energy density, spray-dried microspheres have been shown to achieve high tap densities, which is a significant advantage over loose nanopowders. The spherical morphology naturally leads to better particle packing. By optimizing slurry concentration and sintering conditions, tap densities as high as 1.1–1.4 g/cm³ have been reported for various spray-dried electrode materials, including Li4Ti5O12 and LiFePO4-based composites [ID: 543, 353, 574]. A two-step spray-drying process, where a precursor is first prepared, wet-milled, and then spray-dried, has been used to create dense composite microspheres with high crystallinity [ID: 373]. This suggests that by carefully controlling the precursor preparation and spray-drying parameters, it is possible to fabricate dense NiMoO4 microspheres that still contain sufficient internal nanovoids for strain accommodation.

In summary, the most promising route to a balanced NiMoO4 anode involves using spray drying to assemble primary NiMoO4 nanocrystals into microspheres. To ensure conductivity, a carbon source like sucrose or graphene should be included in the precursor slurry. To create buffering voids, pore-forming agents or a sacrificial template could be added, or a post-annealing step could be designed to induce Kirkendall voiding. Finally, process parameters such as slurry concentration and sintering temperature must be optimized to consolidate the microspheres, achieving a high tap density while preserving the engineered internal porosity and conductive network [ID: 574]. While direct evidence for this specific system is limited, strong analogies from other conversion anodes and spray-dried battery materials provide a clear design and processing pathway.
