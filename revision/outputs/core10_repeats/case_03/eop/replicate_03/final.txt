Based on the experimental situation you've described, it's clear you're facing the classic trade-offs inherent in high-capacity anode materials. The volume expansion is a killer, but solving it by introducing porosity can compromise volumetric energy density and electronic pathways. My approach would be to design a composite material where the architecture itself provides the solution through synergistic effects between the active material and a conductive, structural support.

The goal is to create a hierarchical structure that operates on multiple length scales. We want nano-sized primary particles for fast Li-ion diffusion, assembled into robust, porous microspheres that can be handled easily and achieve a good tap density. Spray drying is an excellent, scalable method for this [ID: 353, 420]. The key is to control the precursor slurry and the drying process to build the desired morphology.

For your NiMoO4 system, I would propose designing a composite microsphere with internal nanovoids and a pervasive carbon network. Here’s how we can break down the design to address your specific bottlenecks, based on the available evidence:

First, to manage the large volume changes during lithiation and delithiation, creating internal void space is the right instinct. This provides a buffer that accommodates the expansion and prevents the pulverization of the microsphere. A recent 2017 paper on multicomponent metal sulfides, including Ni and Mo, successfully used a pilot-scale spray drying process to create microspheres with "empty nanovoids" specifically for this purpose in sodium-ion batteries, which face similar volume expansion issues [ID: 431]. The principle is well-established; for instance, work on SnO2 has shown that hollow nanospheres exhibit far superior cycling and rate performance compared to filled nanoparticles precisely because the void space mitigates structural strain [ID: 461]. This void-based buffering is crucial for durability.

Second, to solve the transport problem of poor electronic conductivity, which is typical for metal oxides like NiMoO4, we must integrate a highly conductive carbon framework. Simply mixing in carbon powders isn't enough; the carbon must be intimately integrated to create a 3D conductive network. A very effective strategy is to introduce a carbon source directly into the spray-drying slurry. For example, graphene-scaffolded Na3V2(PO4)3 microspheres have been made by spray-drying a slurry containing the precursors and graphene oxide [ID: 420]. Upon heat treatment, this creates a porous microsphere where the active nanocrystals are embedded within a continuous graphene network. This architecture provides excellent electronic pathways, leading to fantastic rate performance (good capacity at 50 C) and an incredibly long cycle life of 3000 cycles [ID: 420]. Another approach is a "double carbon coating" process, where primary nanoparticles are first coated with carbon, then assembled into microspheres via spray drying, and the final microsphere is also coated [ID: 353]. This ensures conductivity at both the nano- and micro-scale. This carbon network also acts as a binder, holding the primary particles together and maintaining electrical contact even as the NiMoO4 undergoes conversion reactions [ID: 116].

Third, to balance the need for porosity with the practical requirement of high tap density, a hierarchical structure is the solution. You want porosity *within* the microsphere to facilitate electrolyte penetration and buffer volume changes, but you want the microspheres themselves to be relatively dense and spherical to pack well. Spray drying is well-suited for this. Studies on LiFe0.6Mn0.4PO4/C have shown that spray drying can produce secondary microspheres composed of carbon-coated nanocrystallites, achieving a high tap density of 1.4 g/cm³ [ID: 353]. A similar high tap density was achieved with Li4Ti5O12 microspheres made by an industrial spray-drying approach [ID: 574]. This structure gives you short Li-ion diffusion paths within the small primary particles while the micron-scale spherical morphology ensures good processability and high volumetric energy density. It's a matter of tuning the porosity; one study found that a porosity of up to 20% actually improves rate performance without excessively sacrificing density [ID: 491].

Finally, regarding your process concern about the spray-drying step, the morphology of the final particle (hollow, porous, or solid) is highly dependent on the precursor chemistry and drying kinetics. To avoid collapse and create the desired voids, we can look at a "surface-locking" mechanism [ID: 423]. During spray drying, a rigid crust can form on the droplet surface early in the process, which locks in the spherical shape and can lead to the formation of a hollow or porous interior as the solvent evaporates and the precursors crystallize or decompose. The use of additives in the slurry, such as polymers like PVP or a carbon source like sucrose, can help control this process and also serve as the carbon source for the conductive network upon subsequent calcination [ID: 474, 451]. The work on pitaya-like Sb@C microspheres showed that a robust carbon shell formed during spray pyrolysis can effectively maintain the overall spherical structure through thousands of cycles, even as the internal Sb nanoparticles alloy and de-alloy with sodium [ID: 424].

To put this into a practical synthesis plan, I would suggest starting with a precursor solution containing nickel nitrate, ammonium molybdate [ID: 6], and a dispersed carbon source like graphene oxide. This slurry would be spray-dried to form precursor microspheres. A subsequent heat treatment under an inert or reducing atmosphere would simultaneously crystallize the NiMoO4 and reduce the graphene oxide to form the conductive network. This facile, one-pot spray-drying approach has been successfully used to create similar hierarchical structures for other battery materials [ID: 374, 420] and appears highly promising for producing a durable, high-rate, and high-density NiMoO4 anode.
