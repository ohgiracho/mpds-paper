The core problem you're facing is a classic trade-off in composite electrode design, amplified by the constraints of a high-loading all-solid-state system. With over 85 wt% active material, the volume available for the solid electrolyte (SE) and conductive additive is severely limited. This forces a difficult choice in how to geometrically allocate the LPSCl: prioritize intimate contact at the NCM particle surface or ensure a continuous ionic pathway through the bulk electrode. The challenge is that both are critical, and neglecting one for the other leads to catastrophic failure in transport kinetics.

In this fixed NCM811/LPSCl/conductive-additive system prepared by mechanofusion, a purely surface-biased or interstitial-biased allocation of the limited LPSCl is suboptimal. A surface-biased approach, aiming for a thick, uniform coating on each NCM secondary particle, would ensure excellent local ionic contact. However, with limited SE volume, this would leave insufficient material to fill the interstitial voids between the coated particles. This creates a broken long-range ionic network, effectively isolating regions of the electrode and leading to high tortuosity and poor rate performance, a known bottleneck in thick solid-state cathodes [ID: 157, 524]. Furthermore, a complete coating risks electronically passivating the NCM particles, preventing contact with the conductive additive network, which is just as critical as the ionic network [ID: 20, 421].

Conversely, an interstitial-biased approach using only larger LPSCl particles as fillers would create a bulk ionic pathway but would fail to establish sufficient ionic contact at the NCM particle surface. This results in large un-contacted active material surface areas, leading to severe Li-ion diffusion limitations *to* the NCM, high polarization, and drastic underutilization of the cathode's capacity, especially at practical current densities [ID: 5, 31].

The most plausible and robust solution is a **hierarchical, bimodal allocation strategy**. This involves engineering the LPSCl particle size distribution and leveraging the mechanofusion process to achieve a specific composite microstructure.

1.  **Fine LPSCl Particles for Partial Surface Coverage:** A fraction of the LPSCl should be composed of fine particles (sub-micron to low-micron) intended to adhere to the surface of the polycrystalline NCM811 secondary spheres during mechanofusion. The goal is not a complete, hermetic coating, but rather a high degree of *partial coverage*. This creates a dense network of ionic "point contacts" on the NCM surface, which is crucial for alleviating the diffusive limitations that become severe at high active material content [ID: 31]. This partial coverage leaves sufficient exposed NCM surface for the conductive additive to establish a percolating electronic network, balancing the often-competing requirements of ionic and electronic access [ID: 20, 325]. Mechanofusion is a dry-coating process well-suited for this, as it can apply mechanical energy to embed smaller particles onto the surface of larger ones [ID: 316, 891].

2.  **Coarser LPSCl Particles for Interstitial Filling:** The remaining fraction of the LPSCl should consist of coarser particles. These particles will not effectively coat the NCM but will instead fill the larger interstitial voids between the NCM/fine-LPSCl agglomerates. This component is essential for building a continuous, low-tortuosity ionic superhighway through the bulk of the thick electrode, connecting the individually-contacted NCM particles to the separator [ID: 157, 524]. Using a mix of particle sizes has been shown to improve packing density and overall electrochemical performance in sulfide-based composite cathodes [ID: 345]. This improved packing is also critical for maintaining mechanical integrity and contact under the low stack pressures relevant to practical cells [ID: 58, 252].

In this hierarchical design, you are essentially creating two interconnected ionic networks operating at different length scales. The fine LPSCl provides the local, last-mile connection to the active material, while the coarser LPSCl provides the long-range, low-resistance pathway through the electrode. The mechanofusion process must be carefully optimized to provide enough energy to facilitate the partial coating by the fine particles without fracturing the NCM secondary spheres or excessively pulverizing the coarser LPSCl fillers [ID: 314, 505].

This balanced, bimodal strategy provides the most pragmatic path forward. It directly addresses the dual transport bottlenecks present in a high-loading cathode: the interface to the active material and the bulk transport through the electrode thickness. It avoids the failure modes of the extreme surface- or interstitial-biased approaches by recognizing that a functional composite cathode requires a carefully engineered percolation of both ionic and electronic pathways.
