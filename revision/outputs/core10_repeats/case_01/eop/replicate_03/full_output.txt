Based on the evidence provided, here is my assessment of the microstructural design problem you've presented.

The core of your question is a classic trade-off in composite electrode design, particularly acute in all-solid-state systems where we can't rely on a liquid electrolyte to wet every surface. At high active material loadings of over 85 wt%, every component is fighting for limited space. You have a fixed budget of LPSCl solid electrolyte (SE) and need to decide how to spend it: on coating the NCM811 active material (CAM) particles or on filling the gaps between them. This is not just a geometric puzzle; it is a fundamental challenge of simultaneously creating percolating ionic and electronic networks while minimizing porosity and respecting chemo-mechanical constraints.

A purely surface-biased approach, where you would use very fine LPSCl particles to create a uniform, thick coating on each NCM811 secondary sphere, seems attractive at first. This strategy maximizes the direct CAM-SE interfacial area, which is critical for reducing diffusive limitations and ensuring Li-ions can access the active material [ID: 31]. Mechanofusion is certainly capable of producing such core-shell structures [ID: 891]. However, this approach is fundamentally flawed because it risks creating an electronically insulating shell around the NCM811 particles. If the LPSCl coating is too complete, it will isolate the CAM from the conductive additive network. Electrons would then face a highly tortuous path, needing to find sparse point contacts between NCM particles or through the electronically insulating SE itself. This is analogous to the problem of using excessive nano-sized carbon additives, which can increase interfacial resistance and ultimately harm performance despite improving bulk conductivity [ID: 421, 325]. A perfect ionic interface at the expense of the electronic one is a losing proposition.

Conversely, a purely interstitial-biased strategy is even more problematic. In this scenario, you would use larger LPSCl particles to fill the voids between NCM811 spheres, leaving the CAM surfaces relatively bare. This would create excellent contact between the NCM811 and the conductive additive, establishing a robust electronic network. However, the ionic network would be catastrophically poor. Large areas of the NCM811 surface would be ionically isolated, covered by "voids" from an ionic perspective [ID: 5]. Li-ions would be forced to travel through a highly tortuous path in the interstitial SE before finding a limited number of contact points on the CAM surface. This high tortuosity leads to severe lithiation gradients, especially in thick electrodes, and is a well-known cause of poor rate capability and underutilization of the active material [ID: 524, 157]. Evidence strongly suggests that insufficient CAM-SE contact is a primary bottleneck [ID: 31].

Therefore, the most plausible and effective strategy is a balanced, bimodal approach that engineers both the surface and the interstitial space simultaneously. This requires tailoring the particle size distribution of the LPSCl electrolyte.

My proposed solution is as follows:

1.  **Partial Surface Coverage with Fine LPSCl:** A fraction of the LPSCl should be processed into fine particles, likely on the sub-micron scale. During mechanofusion, these fine particles will preferentially adhere to the NCM811 secondary spheres, creating a *partial* but percolating ionic network across the CAM surface [ID: 891, 505]. The key here is that the coverage is not a complete, hermetic seal. It is a network of SE islands that provides widespread ionic access without completely blocking electronic access. This ensures that a significant portion of the NCM surface remains available for contact with the conductive additive.

2.  **Interstitial Filling with Coarser LPSCl:** The remaining LPSCl should consist of larger, perhaps micron-sized, particles. These larger particles will efficiently fill the interstitial voids between the partially-coated NCM811 spheres. This serves two critical functions. First, it connects the patches of fine SE on adjacent NCM particles, completing the continuous, long-range ionic transport pathway through the bulk of the electrode and lowering the overall ionic tortuosity [ID: 157, 524]. Second, using a bimodal particle size distribution is a well-established materials science principle for increasing packing density, which is absolutely essential for achieving the >85 wt% CAM target without introducing excessive, performance-killing porosity. Evidence from a study using mixed-particle-sized solid electrolytes supports the idea that this heterogeneity can improve electrochemical performance [ID: 345].

This bimodal strategy creates two intertwined, percolating networks. The fine LPSCl provides localized ionic access at the particle surface, mitigating the diffusive limitations that plague high-loading cathodes [ID: 31]. The coarser LPSCl provides the bulk ionic "superhighways" and ensures high packing density. Crucially, the incomplete nature of the surface coating leaves sufficient exposed NCM surface to allow the fixed conductive additive to form its own percolating electronic network, bridging between the active particles.

Of course, this approach is not without its challenges. The precise ratio of fine-to-coarse LPSCl and the optimal particle sizes will require careful empirical optimization of the mechanofusion process, such as milling time and energy [ID: 505]. Furthermore, even with an ideal initial microstructure, the chemo-mechanical degradation of NCM811 upon cycling—anisotropic volume changes leading to particle cracking and contact loss—remains an inherent risk [ID: 58, 252, 545]. The soft, compliant nature of the LPSCl sulfide electrolyte helps to accommodate some of this stress, but it is a failure mode that must always be considered in these systems. Finally, the electrochemical stability of LPSCl itself at the high operating voltages required for NCM811 can lead to the formation of resistive interfacial decomposition products over long-term cycling, a known issue with sulfide electrolytes [ID: 512, 528]. However, within the fixed constraints of your system, a bimodal geometric allocation of LPSCl offers the most robust and rational path toward balancing the competing demands of ionic continuity, electronic accessibility, and dense packing.
