### Diagnosis of the Core Problem

The fundamental challenge here is managing three competing transport and structural requirements within a fixed material set and a limited volume:

1.  **Ionic Transport:** For a Li-ion to reach an NCM811 particle deep within the electrode, it needs a continuous, low-tortuosity path through the solid electrolyte (SE), in this case, LPSCl. Any break in this path or poor contact at the NCM/LPSCl interface creates an "ionically dead" zone, rendering that part of the active material useless, especially at higher C-rates [ID: 5, 57]. This is exacerbated in thick, high-loading electrodes [ID: 157, 321].

2.  **Electronic Transport:** The NCM particles must be in good electronic contact with the conductive additive network. If we smother the NCM particles with an insulating layer—even an ionically conductive one like LPSCl—we can block the electron pathway [ID: 20, 325]. This leads to massive polarization and kills rate capability. The interplay between the ionic and electronic networks is critical and often antagonistic [ID: 20].

3.  **Packing and Mechanical Integrity:** Voids are the enemy of energy density. We need to pack these components as densely as possible. However, the NCM811 particles will expand and contract during cycling, introducing chemo-mechanical stress [ID: 213]. This is particularly problematic for polycrystalline NCM, which is prone to intergranular micro-cracking [ID: 58, 152, 385]. This stress can cause delamination and loss of the fragile solid-solid contact, leading to rapid capacity fade [ID: 252].

Your question boils down to this: with a finite budget of LPSCl, where do we put it to best satisfy these three conflicting demands?

### Proposed Solution: A Bimodal LPSCl Particle Size Strategy

A single, uniform particle size for the LPSCl is a simplistic approach that will inevitably compromise one of the critical requirements. A surface-biased strategy using only fine LPSCl particles risks electronic isolation and poor packing between the larger NCM spheres. An interstitial-biased strategy using only coarse LPSCl particles creates massive ionic transport bottlenecks due to poor point-to-point contact [ID: 112].

Therefore, the most pragmatic and effective solution is to employ a **bimodal particle size distribution for the LPSCl solid electrolyte**, allocating it strategically to perform two distinct functions. This is not about finding a single silver-bullet material, but about the clever combination of morphologies.

#### 1. The Role of Fine LPSCl Particles (Surface Decoration)

A fraction of the LPSCl should be processed into fine, sub-micron particles. During mechanofusion, these fine particles will preferentially adhere to the surfaces of the larger, secondary NCM811 microspheres.

*   **Function:** The goal here is **not** to form a complete, uniform, insulating coating. Instead, we aim for **surface decoration**. This creates a high density of NCM-LPSCl interfaces, ensuring that a large surface area of the active material is ionically accessible [ID: 31]. This is crucial for minimizing charge-transfer resistance.
*   **Why not a full coating?** A complete coating would create an electronically insulating barrier between the NCM and the conductive additive, severely limiting rate performance [ID: 20, 325, 421]. By only decorating the surface, we leave ample exposed area for the conductive additive to form a percolating electronic network.

#### 2. The Role of Coarse LPSCl Particles (Interstitial Ionic Highway)

The remaining, larger fraction of the LPSCl should consist of micron-sized particles, comparable in size to the voids between the NCM secondary particles.

*   **Function:** These coarse particles will primarily fill the interstitial spaces between the NCM spheres and the conductive additive. Their role is to form a continuous, low-tortuosity "ionic highway" that efficiently transports Li-ions from the separator interface deep into the thick electrode [ID: 345]. This bulk network is essential for utilizing the active material in a thick electrode configuration [ID: 157].
*   **Benefit to Packing:** Using a bimodal distribution is a well-established materials science principle for increasing packing density. The smaller particles fill the voids between the larger ones, minimizing dead volume and maximizing the volumetric energy density of the final electrode [ID: 5, 345].

### Justification and Expected Outcome

This bimodal strategy directly addresses the core trade-offs:

*   **Ionic/Electronic Balance:** It establishes both local ionic access at the particle surface (via fine particles) and a long-range ionic network through the bulk (via coarse particles), without electronically isolating the active material. This prioritizes the interplay between the two conductive networks [ID: 20].
*   **Chemo-Mechanical Stability:** The mixture of particle sizes creates a more robust, interpenetrating composite structure. The larger interstitial particles can act as a mechanical scaffold, while the softer nature of the sulfide electrolyte helps buffer some of the volume changes from the NCM811 particles [ID: 10, 252]. This is superior to a thin, brittle, uniform coating that would likely fracture under stress.
*   **Process Compatibility:** This approach is well-suited for mechanofusion. By pre-mixing the bimodal LPSCl with the NCM and conductive additive, a single processing step can achieve this complex microstructure. Control over milling energy and time will be critical to avoid excessive particle fracture of the SE, which can alter its structure and reduce conductivity [ID: 314].

    *   **Rate Capability:** This will immediately expose the flaws of the surface-biased (electronic limitation) and interstitial-biased (ionic limitation) approaches. I expect the bimodal sample to show vastly superior performance.

In conclusion, instead of forcing a single morphology to solve a multi-faceted problem, we should engineer a composite microstructure. A bimodal particle size distribution for the LPSCl is the most logical and robust strategy to balance the competing demands of ionic, electronic, and mechanical properties in a high-loading, all-solid-state NCM811 cathode.
