### Diagnosis of the Problem

The core challenge in fabricating a high-performance, high-loading (>85 wt%) polycrystalline NCM811/LPSCl composite cathode lies in the fundamental trade-off between ionic and electronic transport networks within a limited volume [ID: 20, 42].

1.  **Poor Solid-Solid Contact and High Tortuosity:** Unlike liquid electrolytes that can wet surfaces, rigid solid electrolyte (SE) particles like LPSCl form discrete point contacts with the NCM811 active material (AM) [ID: 5, 25]. At high AM loading, the limited amount of SE results in insufficient AM-SE contact area and an inhomogeneous particle distribution. This creates highly tortuous pathways for Li-ion diffusion, leading to significant diffusive limitations and poor rate capability [ID: 5, 31, 157]. Voids resulting from manufacturing or volume changes during cycling further exacerbate this issue by limiting performance [ID: 5].

2.  **Competing Transport Networks:** The ionic network (provided by LPSCl) and the electronic network (provided by the conductive additive) are in direct competition for space and surface area on the NCM811 particles [ID: 20]. Increasing the SE content or coverage to improve ionic conductivity can disrupt the electronic pathways, increasing the electrode's internal resistance [ID: 10, 421]. Conversely, prioritizing the electronic network can leave large regions of the active material ionically isolated, rendering them electrochemically inaccessible [ID: 31].

3.  **Chemo-mechanical Degradation:** Polycrystalline NCM811 particles are prone to microcracking due to anisotropic volume changes during cycling [ID: 58, 213, 385]. These cracks can sever the ionic and electronic pathways, leading to contact loss and accelerated capacity fade [ID: 58]. A robust initial microstructure is critical to mitigate this degradation.

Therefore, simply maximizing SE surface coverage on the NCM811 or maximizing the filling of interstitial voids represents a false dichotomy. A successful strategy must simultaneously create a continuous ionic pathway to the AM surface, a percolating electronic network, and a dense, mechanically stable electrode structure with low tortuosity [ID: 11].

### Proposed Solution

The most effective strategy to balance these competing requirements is to employ a **bimodal particle size distribution for the LPSCl solid electrolyte**. This approach allocates fine SE particles for surface coverage and coarse SE particles for interstitial filling, creating a hierarchical and efficient transport architecture.

1.  **Fine LPSCl Particles for AM Surface Coverage:** A fraction of the LPSCl should be composed of fine particles. During mechanofusion, these smaller particles will preferentially adhere to the surfaces of the larger NCM811 secondary particles. This directly addresses the critical bottleneck of interfacial charge transfer by maximizing the active AM-SE contact area [ID: 31, 252]. Employing a fine-particle catholyte has been shown to significantly increase the contact area, which alleviates diffusive limitations and improves rate performance [ID: 31]. This ensures that the NCM811 surface is ionically "active."

2.  **Coarse LPSCl Particles for Interstitial Network and Densification:** The remaining, larger fraction of the LPSCl should consist of coarser particles. These larger particles will efficiently fill the interstitial voids between the packed NCM811 microspheres. This serves two critical functions:
    *   It establishes a continuous, long-range ionic conduction network throughout the bulk of the thick electrode, connecting disparate NCM811 particles and ensuring Li-ion transport from the separator to the current collector [ID: 345].
    *   It improves the overall packing density of the composite cathode. A mixture of particle sizes is known to pack more efficiently than uniformly sized particles, which reduces porosity and overall electrode tortuosity [ID: 5, 11]. Research on sulfide-based composites has demonstrated that mixed-particle-sized solid electrolytes lead to superior electrochemical performance compared to uniform-sized electrolytes [ID: 345].

This bimodal approach avoids the drawbacks of a unimodal strategy. Using only fine SE particles would create a highly porous structure and require a greater volume of SE to fill the interstitial spaces, which would severely impede the electronic network and lower the volumetric energy density [ID: 421, 463]. Conversely, using only coarse SE particles would result in poor surface coverage on the NCM811, leading to large ionically inaccessible domains and high polarization [ID: 31].

By allocating fine LPSCl to the AM interface and coarse LPSCl to the interstitial spaces, this strategy creates an optimized microstructure that provides a percolating ionic network at both the particle surface and bulk electrode levels, while leaving sufficient space for the conductive additive to form its own continuous electronic network. This balanced geometric allocation is the most plausible path to preserving ionic continuity, electronic accessibility, and dense packing in the specified high-loading cathode system.

### Recommended Strategy and Path Forward

Based on the evidence and arguments presented, the most defensible and promising strategy is the integrated approach articulated by Scientist B in their final round. This multi-faceted approach addresses the problem's complexity without deviating from our core manufacturing platform. Our research will proceed based on the following framework:

1.  **Adopt a Bimodal Solid Electrolyte (SE) Architecture.** We will not treat the LPSCl as a monolith. The core of our geometric design will be a bimodal particle size distribution. Larger, micron-scale LPSCl particles will be used to fill interstitial voids between NCM811 secondary particles, forming a structural backbone for a low-tortuosity, long-range ionic network. A smaller fraction of fine, sub-micron LPSCl particles will be used to ensure intimate, high-surface-area contact with the NCM811 particles, minimizing charge transfer resistance.

2.  **Engineer a Hybrid, Low-Volume Conductive Network.** To ensure electronic accessibility with minimal side reactions and volume consumption, we will employ a hybrid carbon strategy. The primary network for long-range electron transport will be built using high-aspect-ratio, crystalline carbon nanofibers (CNFs). 3.  **Implement Dry-Process-Compatible Interfacial Stabilization.** Interfacial reactivity is a non-negotiable failure point that must be addressed. We will pursue a two-pronged stabilization strategy that is compatible with our mechanofusion process. First, we will procure or develop NCM811 powders that are pre-coated with a stable, ionically conductive buffer layer (e.g., LiNbO₃). Second, we will investigate the formation of a self-compatible artificial cathode-electrolyte interphase (CEI) via a dry-coating or mild heat-treatment step, as this has been shown to suppress degradation without requiring wet chemistry.

4.  **Optimize Processing for Densification and Mechanical Integrity.** The final microstructure is a product of both materials and process. We will refine our mechanofusion protocol to achieve the target bimodal SE distribution without excessive amorphization. Critically, we will incorporate a post-fabrication **mild-temperature pressing step (e.g., ~85°C)**. This step is key to increasing the deformability of the LPSCl, allowing it to flow and form dense, robust, and stress-free contacts throughout the entire composite cathode, which enhances both density and mechanical resilience to cycling-induced strain.

This integrated strategy provides the most plausible path to balancing the competing demands of ionic transport, electronic access, and dense packing within the specified high-loading composite system. It leverages geometric design, manages chemical instability, and respects the realities of our manufacturing process.
