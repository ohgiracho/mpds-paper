Based on the evidence provided, a balanced geometric allocation of the limited LPSCl solid electrolyte (SE), utilizing a mix of particle sizes, is the most plausible strategy to simultaneously preserve ionic continuity, electronic accessibility, and dense packing in a high-loading (>85 wt%) polycrystalline NCM811/LPSCl/conductive additive composite cathode prepared by mechanofusion.

A purely surface-biased or interstitial-biased allocation presents significant trade-offs that are likely to compromise overall performance. The optimal approach involves using a controlled particle size distribution of LPSCl to achieve both partial surface coverage of the NCM811 secondary particles and effective filling of the interstitial voids between them.

### Analysis of Geometric Allocation Strategies

**1. The Core Trilemma: Ionic, Electronic, and Packing Demands**

At high active material (AM) loading (>85 wt%), the composite cathode is a crowded environment where ionic conductors (LPSCl), electronic conductors (the fixed conductive additive, CA), and the active material (NCM811) compete for limited volume and interfacial contact.

*   **Ionic Continuity:** Requires intimate, extensive contact between NCM811 and LPSCl particles to minimize Li-ion diffusion limitations. Insufficient contact leads to high interfacial resistance and underutilization of the active material [ID: 5, 31]. The formation of voids at the interface is particularly detrimental [ID: 5].
*   **Electronic Accessibility:** NCM811 has poor intrinsic electronic conductivity [ID: 20]. Therefore, a percolating network of the conductive additive must maintain contact with the NCM811 surface. A thick, passivating layer of insulating SE on the NCM811 surface can block electron transport, increasing charge transfer resistance and limiting rate capability [ID: 509, 515, 421].
*   **Dense Packing:** To achieve high energy density, the volume occupied by inactive components like SE and CA must be minimized. This requires efficient packing of all components and minimizing porosity [ID: 42, 545]. The tortuosity of both ionic and electronic pathways must also be minimized for efficient transport through the thick electrode structure [ID: 157, 524].

**2. Evaluation of Allocation Geometries**

*   **Surface-Biased Allocation (Primarily fine SE particles for coating):**
    This strategy would prioritize maximizing the NCM-SE interfacial area. Using fine SE particles to coat the NCM811 can reduce diffusive limitations at the particle surface, which is a known bottleneck, especially at high AM content [ID: 31]. However, the evidence suggests this approach is suboptimal. A complete, uniform coating of LPSCl would create an electronically insulating barrier between the NCM811 and the conductive additive, severely impeding electronic percolation [ID: 20, 515]. While surface coatings are often beneficial, these are typically ultrathin layers of specific ion-conducting or passivating materials (e.g., LiNbO3, Al2O3) applied by methods like ALD or wet chemistry [ID: 101, 133, 528], which is distinct from using the bulk SE itself as the coating material in a mechanofusion process. Furthermore, if the limited SE is consumed entirely for surface coating, the larger interstitial voids between NCM secondary particles may remain unfilled, leading to discontinuous ionic pathways and high electrode-scale tortuosity [ID: 157].

*   **Interstitial-Biased Allocation (Primarily coarse SE particles for filling):**
    This strategy prioritizes electronic contact by leaving NCM811 surfaces more accessible to the conductive additive. Using larger SE particles to fill the gaps between NCM secondary particles can establish bulk ionic pathways. However, this geometry would result in poor point-to-point contact between the large NCM particles and the large SE filler particles. This lack of sufficient surface contact area would lead to high interfacial resistance, large diffusive limitations, and significant portions of "dead" or underutilized active material, severely compromising capacity and rate performance [ID: 5, 31].

*   **Balanced (Hybrid) Allocation (A mix of fine and coarse SE particles):**
    This strategy appears to be the most effective compromise. It involves using a bimodal or broad particle size distribution of LPSCl, which can be controlled via the mechanofusion process parameters (e.g., milling time) [ID: 505, 316].
    *   **Fine LPSCl particles** would adhere to the NCM811 surface, creating sufficient, but not necessarily complete, ionic contact points. This ensures Li-ions can access the active material without forming a fully insulating shell, thus preserving pathways for electronic contact. Studies show that using a fine-particle catholyte significantly increases the contact area and alleviates diffusive limitations [ID: 31, 252].
    *   **Coarse LPSCl particles** would efficiently fill the larger interstitial voids between the NCM811 secondary particles. This is crucial for creating a continuous, low-tortuosity ionic percolation network throughout the thick electrode, which is essential for high-loading cathodes [ID: 157, 524].

    Direct evidence supports this hybrid approach. A study on sulfide-based cathodes found that a **mixed particle-sized solid electrolyte-based cathode composite exhibited higher specific capacitance** than a uniform-sized one [ID: 345]. This indicates that a distribution of SE particle sizes is beneficial for overall performance. The combination of particle sizes can also lead to better packing density and lower overall porosity compared to monodisperse systems.

### The Role of Processing and Conductive Additive

The choice of mechanofusion as the fabrication method is compatible with creating a controlled particle size distribution and achieving dry coating of particles [ID: 891, 316]. However, the process must be carefully optimized. Excessive milling to produce fine particles can reduce the intrinsic ionic conductivity of the LPSCl itself due to amorphization or structural disorder [ID: 505, 314].

The fixed conductive additive's morphology also plays a critical, albeit unchangeable, role. The electronic and ionic networks are in direct competition for interfacial access [ID: 20, 421]. Evidence suggests that long-aspect-ratio additives like carbon nanofibers (CNF) are more effective at forming robust, long-range electronic pathways with less material compared to point-contact additives like carbon black (CB) [ID: 325, 463]. A balanced SE allocation strategy must ensure that enough NCM surface remains accessible for this fixed CA to form a percolating network.

### Conclusion and Plausible Path Forward

Within the fixed constraints, a **balanced geometric allocation of LPSCl is the most plausible strategy**. This involves engineering the LPSCl particle size distribution through mechanofusion to create a composite microstructure with:

1.  **Partial surface coverage** of polycrystalline NCM811 secondary particles by a population of **fine LPSCl particles** to ensure adequate local ionic access.
2.  **Interstitial filling** between the NCM811 particles by a population of **coarser LPSCl particles** to establish a continuous, low-tortuosity bulk ionic network and maintain pathways for electronic access.

This hybrid approach, supported by direct experimental evidence [ID: 345], provides the best framework to resolve the inherent trade-off between ionic continuity, electronic accessibility, and dense packing. The optimal ratio of fine-to-coarse SE particles and the precise degree of surface coverage are critical parameters that must be determined through systematic experimental optimization of the mechanofusion process. While this strategy is strongly supported by the available evidence, it is important to note that chemo-mechanical degradation, such as cracking of NCM particles and contact loss from volume changes, remains a long-term failure risk that geometric optimization alone may not fully resolve [ID: 58, 213, 545].
