Based on the evidence provided, the optimal geometric allocation of limited LPSCl solid electrolyte (SE) in a mechanofusion-prepared polycrystalline NCM811 composite cathode at >85 wt% active material (CAM) involves a balanced, bimodal distribution strategy. This approach allocates finer LPSCl particles for surface coverage on the NCM811 secondary spheres and coarser LPSCl particles for interstitial filling, aiming to synergistically preserve ionic continuity, electronic accessibility, and dense packing.

At high active material loadings (>85 wt%), the composite cathode faces a fundamental trade-off. Ensuring sufficient ionic transport requires intimate contact between the NCM811 and the LPSCl, which favors extensive surface coverage. However, this can impede electronic pathways by insulating the CAM from the conductive additive network. Conversely, prioritizing interstitial filling to maintain electronic contact risks creating ionically isolated CAM particles with high tortuosity and diffusive limitations [ID: 31, 5].

**Ionic Continuity and Surface Coverage with Fine SE Particles**

Evidence strongly indicates that insufficient CAM-SE contact is a primary bottleneck at high CAM loadings. Diffusive limitations arise from a small contact area between the active material and the solid electrolyte, a problem that is particularly pronounced for cathodes with over 84 wt% active material [ID: 31]. Simulations also highlight the performance degradation caused by voids at the CAM-SE interface, which reduce the active surface area [ID: 5]. To mitigate this, a significant portion of the LPSCl should be dedicated to coating the NCM811 particles.

The particle size of the SE used for coating is critical. Employing fine-particle catholytes has been shown to significantly increase the contact area, which alleviates diffusive limitations and improves rate performance [ID: 31]. Similarly, studies show that decreasing the SE particle size leads to greater CAM-SE contact [ID: 252]. Dry coating processes, analogous to mechanofusion, can successfully produce uniform NCM@LPS core-shell composite particles, which create a larger contact area between the CAM and SE, resulting in improved electrochemical performance [ID: 891]. Therefore, a surface-biased allocation using fine LPSCl particles is essential for establishing a percolating ionic network directly on the active material surfaces.

However, there is a caveat: while smaller SE particles are beneficial for contact and reducing tortuosity, the mechanical milling process used to create them can also reduce the SE's crystallinity and, consequently, its intrinsic ionic conductivity. This suggests an optimal, rather than minimal, particle size must be achieved through controlled processing [ID: 314, 505].

**Electronic Accessibility and Interstitial Space**

The electronic network, composed of the fixed conductive additive, must remain percolated throughout the composite to ensure all CAM particles are accessible. This network primarily occupies the interstitial voids between the larger NCM811 secondary particles. A strategy that fills these voids exclusively with LPSCl would disrupt electronic pathways, leading to high electronic resistance and isolated, underutilized regions of the electrode.

The interplay between the ionic (LPSCl) and electronic (conductive additive) networks is competitive. Studies on sulfide-based cathodes have shown that an excessive amount of nano-sized carbon additives can increase interfacial resistance with the sulfide SE, harming performance more than the enhanced electrical conductivity helps [ID: 325, 421]. This highlights that maximizing one network at the expense of the other is detrimental. A purely surface-coverage strategy, if the coating is too thick, could likewise prevent the NCM811 particles from making contact with the conductive additive in the interstitial spaces, leading to electronic isolation.

**Proposed Optimal Geometry: A Balanced, Bimodal Allocation**

The evidence points toward a dual-pronged geometric strategy that leverages a bimodal particle size distribution for the LPSCl. This approach avoids the pitfalls of the extreme "surface-only" or "interstitial-only" allocations.

1.  **Fine LPSCl for Surface Coverage:** A fraction of the LPSCl should be processed into fine particles, through optimized mechanofusion conditions that balance size reduction with preserving crystallinity [ID: 314, 505]. These fine particles should be used to form a thin, uniform coating on the NCM811 secondary spheres. This directly addresses the need for high CAM-SE contact area and low ionic tortuosity at the particle level [ID: 31, 252, 891].

2.  **Coarser LPSCl for Interstitial Filling:** The remaining LPSCl, as larger particles, should be mixed with the conductive additive to fill the interstitial voids between the coated NCM811 particles. The use of mixed particle-sized SE in the composite has been shown to yield higher specific capacitance than using uniformly sized SE [ID: 345]. This bimodal distribution contributes to higher packing density, which is critical for achieving >85 wt% CAM loading. The larger interstitial SE particles help fill large voids and establish long-range ionic pathways between CAM particles, while leaving sufficient space for the conductive additive to form a continuous electronic network [ID: 325, 511]. This prevents the electronic network from being completely disrupted by the ion-conducting phase.

In summary, to best preserve ionic continuity, electronic accessibility, and dense packing, the limited LPSCl should be geometrically allocated using a bimodal size distribution. Fine LPSCl particles should be dedicated to forming a thin, uniform coating on the polycrystalline NCM811 secondary particles to ensure low-resistance ionic pathways at the particle surface. The remainder of the LPSCl, as coarser particles, should be co-located with the conductive additive in the interstitial spaces. This balanced strategy, supported by evidence favoring fine particles for contact [ID: 31], mixed particle sizes for overall performance [ID: 345], and the necessity of maintaining separate, percolating ionic and electronic networks [ID: 325, 421], represents the most plausible approach to optimizing the composite cathode under the given constraints.
