This is a fascinating and highly relevant challenge you're tackling. Crafting a synergistic composite from PBA-derived hollow sulfides and reduced graphene oxide (rGO) for sodium-ion battery anodes is precisely the kind of nanoscale architectural engineering that holds immense promise for next-generation energy storage. The bottlenecks you've identified—structural collapse, electrolyte blocking, and delamination—are common when trying to harness the high theoretical capacity of conversion materials. The key, as I see it, is to move beyond simple mixing and design a truly integrated, hierarchical structure where each component's strengths address the other's weaknesses.

Based on my experience with MOF-derived nanostructures and graphene composites, I would propose an approach centered on using the hollow sulfide nanocubes themselves as both the active material and as structural "spacers" to build a three-dimensional, porous rGO framework. This prevents the rGO sheets from restacking, which is a critical issue that often chokes ion pathways, while simultaneously creating a robust, conductive network that encases the active material.

Here is a design and synthesis strategy to consider:

First, let's focus on the synthesis, as the final architecture is a direct result of the process chemistry. Rather than preparing the components separately and then mixing them, an in-situ, one-pot approach will likely yield a more intimately coupled composite. I would suggest starting with a stable aqueous dispersion of graphene oxide (GO) and your as-synthesized PBA nanocube templates. The abundant oxygen-containing functional groups on the GO sheets can interact with the surface of the PBA cubes, possibly through electrostatic forces or coordination, ensuring the cubes are well-distributed and preventing them from agglomerating during subsequent processing [ID: 212, 489].

The next step is the crucial transformation. A hydrothermal or solvothermal reaction is an excellent method for this. In a single step, you can achieve both the sulfidation of the PBA template and the reduction of GO to rGO. For the sulfidation, you can introduce a sulfur source like Na₂S into the reaction medium. This anion-exchange process has been shown to be effective in converting PBA nanocubes into hollow nickel sulfide nanoframes while preserving the essential morphology of the template [ID: 1, 2]. The key is to control the reaction kinetics to favor the hollowing-out process, which relies on the differential reactivity of the cube's faces versus its edges and corners, rather than forming solid particles. Gaseous sulfurization is another powerful technique, but integrating it with the GO reduction in a single step is more complex [ID: 15].

Simultaneously, the hydrothermal conditions will reduce the GO to rGO [ID: 157]. You can even tune this process. A partial reduction might be advantageous, as the remaining oxygen functional groups on the rGO can serve as anchoring sites for the newly formed sulfide nanocrystals [ID: 506]. This creates a stronger, more durable interface than simple physical mixing, which is vital for preventing the sulfide particles from detaching and becoming inactive during the large volume changes associated with the sodiation/desodiation conversion reaction [ID: 593]. This directly addresses your concern about the architecture separating into fragments.

The resulting composite architecture should be a three-dimensional network where the hollow, porous sulfide nanocubes are effectively "pillared" between the flexible rGO sheets [ID: 211]. This structure elegantly solves your primary bottlenecks:

1.  **Structural Robustness:** The rGO sheets act as a flexible, conductive matrix that wraps around the sulfide nanocubes. This resilient web can accommodate the significant volume expansion and contraction of the sulfide during cycling, preventing pulverization and maintaining electrical contact throughout the electrode [ID: 258, 362, 593].

2.  **Ion and Electron Transport:** The interconnected network of rGO sheets provides continuous, long-range pathways for electrons, ensuring high conductivity across the entire electrode [ID: 150]. At the same time, because the sulfide nanocubes are acting as spacers, they prevent the rGO sheets from restacking into an impermeable film. This maintains an open, porous structure with high surface area, allowing the electrolyte to freely access the porous walls and interior of the hollow sulfide cubes [ID: 231, 54]. This dual-pathway design for both ions and electrons is critical for achieving high rate capability.

Regarding your process concern about spray pyrolysis, while it is a scalable method, it can be challenging to achieve the uniform, nanoscale integration we're discussing. The one-pot hydrothermal approach I've outlined offers more precise control over the self-assembly process, promoting the in-situ formation of a hierarchical composite rather than a simple mixture of separate sulfide particles and rGO aggregates.

In essence, the goal is to create a composite that is more than the sum of its parts. The hollow sulfide provides the high capacity for sodium storage, and the rGO provides the electronic conductivity and mechanical integrity needed to actually realize that capacity over many cycles. The challenges you're facing are not fundamental limitations but rather engineering problems of nano-architecture. By carefully controlling the synthesis to build this integrated, pillared 3D structure, I am optimistic that you can overcome these bottlenecks and develop a truly high-performance anode material for sodium-ion batteries.
