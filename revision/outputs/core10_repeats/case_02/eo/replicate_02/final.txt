Based on the provided evidence, a promising design for a multicomponent Mo/Ni chalcogenide microsphere anode would be a hierarchical, porous composite architecture integrating the active materials with a conductive carbon scaffold, such as reduced graphene oxide (rGO). This design can be fabricated using a scalable spray-drying process followed by a post-treatment step.

Metal chalcogenides are considered promising anode materials for sodium-ion batteries (SIBs) due to their high theoretical capacities, but they often suffer from low electronic conductivity and significant volume expansion during sodiation, which leads to structural collapse and poor cycling stability [ID: 11]. A multicomponent approach, combining molybdenum and nickel chalcogenides, could leverage the complementary properties of different materials, a strategy that has been shown to be effective in lithium-ion batteries (LIBs) for balancing capacity and rate performance [ID: 3]. While direct evidence for a specific Mo/Ni chalcogenide compound for SIBs is not prominent in the provided literature, MoS₂ has demonstrated reversible sodium storage [ID: 14, 69, 75], and other multicomponent transition metal chalcogenides like Cu₂NiSnS₄ have shown good performance as SIB anodes [ID: 11]. Therefore, a composite of Mo- and Ni-based chalcogenides is a rational starting point.

To address the challenges of poor conductivity and structural instability, the active chalcogenide phases should be integrated into a hierarchical microsphere structure with an rGO framework. The proposed architecture would consist of spherical secondary particles, approximately 5–15 µm in diameter, which are themselves aggregates of primary nanosheet or nanoparticle building blocks [ID: 523, 607]. This hierarchical design offers several advantages. The micron-sized spherical morphology provides good tap density, which is crucial for practical volumetric energy density, and improves processability compared to loose nanomaterials [ID: 520, 683]. The internal structure, composed of assembled nanosheets, creates a mesoporous network that facilitates electrolyte penetration and provides short diffusion paths for Na⁺ ions, thereby enhancing accessibility to the active material [ID: 11, 75].

Incorporating rGO is critical for maintaining electronic continuity and providing structural buffering. The rGO sheets can form an interconnected, three-dimensional conductive network throughout the microsphere, wrapping the chalcogenide nanoparticles and ensuring efficient electron transport [ID: 11, 523, 539]. This rGO matrix also acts as a flexible buffer to accommodate the significant volume changes during sodiation/desodiation, preventing pulverization of the active material and maintaining the integrity of the electrode structure over repeated cycles [ID: 69, 86, 620].

To create the desired internal nanovoids for strain accommodation without sacrificing excessive packing density, a hollow or porous core within the microsphere is advantageous. Such voids provide space to buffer volume expansion [ID: 86, 614]. This can be achieved through a scalable two-step process involving spray drying and subsequent thermal treatment. Spray drying is a high-throughput method capable of producing spherical precursor particles [ID: 583, 675]. A slurry containing the precursors—such as Mo and Ni salts, a chalcogen source like thiourea, and graphene oxide (GO)—can be spray-dried to form composite microspheres [ID: 523, 539, 587]. This one-pot precursor synthesis ensures a homogeneous distribution of components.

Following spray drying, a post-treatment step, such as calcination or sulfidation in an inert or reducing atmosphere, would convert the precursors into the desired Mo/Ni chalcogenide phases and simultaneously reduce GO to rGO [ID: 137, 523, 664]. This heat treatment can also be designed to generate internal voids. For instance, the decomposition of organic binders or precursors used in the spray-drying slurry can create porosity [ID: 607, 675]. Alternatively, a nanoscale Kirkendall diffusion process during the thermal conversion step can be used to form hollow nanostructures within the microsphere, as demonstrated for FeSeₓ and SnO₂ systems [ID: 86, 614].

In summary, a design featuring hierarchical microspheres composed of Mo/Ni chalcogenide nanosheets intimately wrapped in an rGO network would best meet the stated requirements. This architecture, synthesized via a scalable spray-drying and post-treatment process, effectively preserves sodium-ion accessibility through its porous structure [ID: 11], ensures electronic continuity via the rGO scaffold [ID: 523], and provides structural buffering against volume expansion through internal voids and the flexible carbon matrix [ID: 86, 620], all while maintaining a relatively high packing density suitable for practical applications.
