### The Flaw in the "Scaffold": Containing an Explosion

Your core premise is that an external carbon matrix can buffer the 300% volume expansion of tin oxide. This is a mechanical fallacy. When a SnO₂ particle lithiates and expands, it exerts immense pressure on the surrounding carbon. This leads to two primary failure modes you conveniently ignore:

1.  **Interfacial Failure:** The bond between the oxide and the carbon matrix, no matter how "intimate," is the weakest link. Repeated, massive strain will cause delamination. Once the particle detaches, it is electrically isolated and becomes dead weight.
2.  **Internal Pulverization:** Even if the carbon matrix holds, the SnO₂ particle itself will still fracture and pulverize *within* its carbon cage. You've simply contained the debris, but you haven't prevented the fundamental degradation process that severs electrical pathways within the active material itself.

My approach, creating a hollow structure via the Kirkendall effect, is far more mechanically sound. The void is not an "eggshell"; it is an engineered expansion chamber [ID: 39, 94]. The volume change is directed *inward*, into the void, dramatically reducing the external stress on the particle and its neighbors [ID: 66]. This is not a theory; it is a demonstrated principle. A direct comparison of hollow vs. dense Fe₂O₃ nanospheres showed that the hollow structures retained 92% capacity after 200 cycles, while the dense particles retained only 45% [ID: 39]. The morphology is what matters.
