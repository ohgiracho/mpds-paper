Topic: Within a fixed polycrystalline NCM811/LPSCl/existing-conductive-additive composite prepared by mechanofusion, how should limited LPSCl be geometrically allocated between particle-surface coverage and interstitial filling to best preserve ionic continuity, electronic accessibility, and dense packing at >85 wt% active material?
============================================================
[EO Answer]
Based on the provided evidence, the optimal geometric allocation of limited LPSCl solid electrolyte (SE) within a fixed polycrystalline NCM811/LPSCl/conductive-additive composite cathode prepared by mechanofusion should prioritize a balanced, bimodal distribution strategy. This approach involves using a mix of fine LPSCl particles for surface coverage on the NCM811 secondary spheres and larger LPSCl particles for interstitial filling. This configuration most plausibly balances the competing requirements of ionic continuity, electronic accessibility, and dense packing at active material loadings greater than 85 wt%.

The core challenge is a trade-off between establishing percolating ionic and electronic networks within a densely packed structure. At high active material content, the volume available for both the SE and the conductive additive (CA) is severely restricted, making the geometric arrangement of these components critical.

A purely surface-biased allocation, achieved by using only fine SE particles to create a high degree of coverage on the NCM811 particles, would effectively minimize the Li-ion diffusion distance from the SE to the active material surface. This is crucial because increasing the active material to solid electrolyte ratio is known to increase diffusive limitations, which originate from the small contact area between the active material and the SE [ID: 31]. Employing fine particle catholytes can significantly increase this contact area, alleviating the diffusive limitation that becomes particularly pronounced at over 84 wt% active material [ID: 31]. However, a thick or complete coating of ionically conductive but electronically insulating LPSCl can isolate the NCM811 particles from the conductive additive network, thereby increasing charge transfer resistance and hindering electronic accessibility [ID: 42, 64]. This trade-off is a known issue, where the benefits of an electrical network can be overpowered by increased bulk resistance if the conductive additive's interaction with the SE is not managed [ID: 20].

Conversely, a purely interstitial-biased allocation, using larger SE particles to fill the voids between NCM811 spheres while leaving the NCM811 surfaces relatively bare, would prioritize direct contact between the active material and the conductive additive. This would ensure excellent electronic accessibility. However, this geometry would create severe ionic transport bottlenecks. The limited points of contact between the NCM811 particles and the large interstitial SE fillers would drastically reduce the active surface area available for electrochemical reaction and create highly tortuous pathways for Li-ion transport [ID: 5, 157]. Such high tortuosity significantly impedes ion transport, especially in thick electrodes, and leads to heterogeneous reactions and poor active material utilization [ID: 157, 524].

A balanced, bimodal strategy using both fine and coarse LPSCl particles offers the most promising solution. In this configuration:
1.  **Fine LPSCl particles** are used to achieve partial or thin coverage on the NCM811 secondary spheres during mechanofusion. This establishes intimate ionic contact at the active material surface, addressing the primary diffusive limitation [ID: 31]. Mechanofusion is a suitable dry-coating technology for creating such core-shell structures [ID: 316, 891].
2.  **Larger LPSCl particles** simultaneously fill the interstitial voids between the coated NCM811 particles. This serves two purposes: it improves the overall packing density of the composite cathode and creates continuous, less tortuous bulk ionic pathways throughout the electrode volume [ID: 345, 505]. Studies on composite cathodes have shown that using mixed particle sizes for the solid electrolyte can lead to better electrochemical performance compared to using uniformly sized particles [ID: 345].

This bimodal approach creates an optimized microstructure with percolating networks for both ions and electrons. The fine SE particles ensure local ionic access to the NCM811, while the larger particles form the backbone of the bulk ionic network. The conductive additive can still form a percolating electronic network through the remaining bare surface areas on the NCM811 and the contact points between the larger particles. The evidence suggests that optimizing the particle size of the SE is critical, as processing can reduce crystallite size and decrease intrinsic ionic conductivity, but smaller particles can also lower the tortuosity in the final composite [ID: 314, 505]. A bimodal distribution leverages the benefits of both small and large particles.

This strategy is particularly relevant for the specified polycrystalline NCM811 platform. These secondary particles are known to be susceptible to chemo-mechanical degradation and intergranular cracking during cycling [ID: 58, 213, 570]. A well-distributed, bimodal SE network that provides both surface and interstitial ionic pathways can help maintain ionic access to primary grains even if microcracks begin to form, potentially mitigating the performance decline associated with particle fracture.

While the provided evidence strongly supports the principles behind a bimodal SE allocation, it is important to note that direct studies systematically optimizing the fine-to-coarse LPSCl ratio within a mechanofused NCM811 composite are not present in the snapshot. The optimal ratio would likely depend on the precise morphologies and size distributions of the NCM811, LPSCl, and the fixed conductive additive, requiring experimental fine-tuning of the mechanofusion process to achieve the desired microstructure without causing mechanical damage to the NCM811 secondary spheres.
