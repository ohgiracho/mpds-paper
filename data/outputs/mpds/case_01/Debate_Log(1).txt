- Background:
 We are optimizing a high-loading sulfide ASSB cathode based on fixed polycrystalline NCM811 secondary microspheres, fixed LPSCl, and a fixed existing conductive additive under mechanofusion. The main design levers are CAM–SE coverage degree, how limited LPSCl is allocated between surface coverage and interstitial filling, SE particle-size distribution, and mechanofusion processing conditions within this fixed composite.

- Main bottleneck:
 At >85 wt% active material, insufficient or non-uniform CAM–SE contact can reduce ionic continuity and increase transport tortuosity. However, increasing SE coverage or SE content can also reduce electronic accessibility and lower electrode density.

- Transport bottleneck:
 The central question is how limited LPSCl should be split between (i) surface coverage on the polycrystalline NCM811 secondary particles and (ii) interstitial filling between particles. Both the surface-coverage fraction and the filler fraction must remain LPSCl. CAM–SE coverage degree and LPSCl particle-size allocation jointly affect ionic continuity, electronic accessibility, and microstructural tortuosity. The optimal balance is not known in advance and must be reasoned out.

 The original polycrystalline NCM811 secondary-microsphere morphology must remain intact during mechanofusion. The answer should preserve this fixed polycrystalline secondary-particle platform rather than replacing it with single-crystal NCM, alternative cathode morphologies, new active-material platforms, or alternative electrolyte chemistries.

[The Architect (Scientist A) - Round 1]
Based on the data and my experience at the bench, your problem is a classic percolation and packing challenge, not a materials discovery issue. You have a fixed budget of ionic conductor (LPSCl) and you need to build both the local "access roads" (surface coverage) and the "interstate highways" (interstitial pathways) within your cathode composite. At over 85 wt% active material, you're operating in a conductor-starved regime where every geometric choice has severe consequences.

### Diagnosis of the Core Problem

The fundamental trade-off is between interfacial impedance at the particle level and tortuosity at the electrode level.

1.  **The Peril of Poor Surface Coverage:** If you prioritize filling the interstitial voids with larger LPSCl particles, you leave significant portions of the NCM811 secondary particle surface bare. An ion cannot jump from an LPSCl particle across a void or a carbon particle to the NCM811 surface. These uncovered areas become electrochemically dead zones. This leads to a dramatic reduction in active material utilization and creates localized current hotspots on the few areas that *do* have contact, accelerating degradation. This is a diffusive limitation originating from the small contact area between the active material and the solid electrolyte [ID: 31].

2.  **The Peril of Insufficient Interstitial Filling:** Conversely, if you use only fine LPSCl to create a conformal coating on the NCM811 particles, you solve the local contact problem. However, with a limited total volume of SE, the large interstitial voids between the secondary spheres will be poorly filled. Unlike a liquid electrolyte that wets and fills everything, a solid composite leaves voids that are ion-blocking [ID: 5]. This creates an extremely tortuous path for ions to travel from the separator interface deep into the thick electrode, leading to massive polarization and poor rate capability.

3.  **The Electronic Network Complication:** Both scenarios are complicated by the fixed conductive additive. A thick, uniform coating of LPSCl can insulate the NCM811 from the electronic conductor, increasing charge transfer resistance. The interplay between the electrical and ionic networks is paramount, and prioritizing one at the total expense of the other is a recipe for failure [ID: 20].

### Proposed Solution: A Bimodal LPSCl Particle Size Distribution

A single particle size for the LPSCl is a flawed approach. The optimal geometric allocation requires a bimodal particle size distribution, carefully processed via mechanofusion. This strategy has been shown to yield better performance than using uniform-sized particles [ID: 345].

Here is the proposed microstructure and processing logic:

1.  **Fine LPSCl Fraction (for Surface Coverage):**
    *   **Material:** A fraction of the total LPSCl budget should be pre-milled to a fine particle size (sub-micron, ideally).
    *   **Function:** This fine powder will act as the primary agent for surface coverage. During mechanofusion, these smaller, higher-surface-energy particles will preferentially adhere to and deform onto the surfaces of the larger NCM811 secondary spheres. This directly addresses the need for high active surface area contact, minimizing the diffusive limitations that plague high-loading cathodes [ID: 31, 252]. This creates the "access roads."

2.  **Coarse LPSCl Fraction (for Interstitial Filling):**
    *   **Material:** The remaining fraction of the LPSCl should consist of larger, micron-scale particles.
    *   **Function:** These larger particles are dimensionally suited to efficiently fill the interstitial voids between the ~10 µm NCM811 secondary spheres. They will form a percolating, low-tortuosity network for rapid ion transport through the bulk of the electrode. This creates the "interstate highways" [ID: 5].

3.  **Controlled Mechanofusion Protocol:**
    *   The process cannot be a simple one-step dump-and-mill. A sequential or controlled-energy approach is necessary to build the desired architecture without destroying the components.
    *   **Step 1 (Adhesion):** Begin with a lower-energy mechanofusion step, mixing the NCM811 with only the *fine* LPSCl fraction. The goal is to promote adhesion and surface coverage without fracturing the polycrystalline NCM811 secondary particles [ID: 58].
    *   **Step 2 (Densification):** Introduce the *coarse* LPSCl fraction and the fixed conductive additive. Apply a second, carefully calibrated mechanofusion step. The energy should be sufficient to deform the soft sulfide particles and densify the composite, filling the voids, but low enough to avoid excessive particle fracture of the NCM811 or amorphization of the LPSCl, which can reduce its intrinsic conductivity [ID: 314].

This bimodal strategy creates a composite where the NCM811 particles are adequately wetted by the fine LPSCl for good interfacial kinetics, while the bulk of the electrode is filled with coarse LPSCl to ensure an efficient ion transport network.

### Risks and Necessary Verifications

Even with this optimized geometry, risks remain. This is not a magic bullet.

*   **Electronic Percolation:** The fine LPSCl coating, while necessary, will compete with the conductive additive for surface contact on the NCM811. The final structure will be a complex, interpenetrating network of all three components. You must verify that the electronic conductivity is not catastrophically compromised. Impedance spectroscopy separating charge transfer and bulk ionic/electronic resistance is non-negotiable here.
*   **Chemo-Mechanical Stability:** NCM811 undergoes volume changes during cycling. While the soft nature of LPSCl is advantageous compared to rigid oxides, contact loss and micro-void formation at the interface upon cycling are inevitable [ID: 252]. The low stack pressure of a coin cell makes this particularly challenging [ID: 58]. Post-mortem cross-sectional SEM/EDX is required to assess the integrity of the interfaces after extended cycling.
*   **Sulfide Reactivity:** The interface between NCM811 and LPSCl is not perfectly stable, especially at higher voltages [ID: 415, 339]. While we are not adding a new coating layer, the increased interfacial area from the fine LPSCl fraction could potentially increase the total amount of interfacial degradation products over time. XPS analysis of the cycled cathode is necessary to understand the evolution of this interface.

In conclusion, a bimodal LPSCl particle size distribution is the most rational approach to balance the competing demands of interfacial contact and bulk transport within your fixed material set. It is an engineering solution to a geometric problem, but its success is entirely dependent on meticulous process control and rigorous electrochemical and microscopic verification.
