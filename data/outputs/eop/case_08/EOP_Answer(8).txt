[EOP Answer]
Based on your experimental situation, the core challenge is architectural. We need to design a composite where the reduced graphene oxide (rGO) acts as a flexible, conductive scaffold that supports the hollow sulfide nanocubes without smothering them. The goal is to create a stable, three-dimensional porous network that maintains both electronic and ionic pathways throughout cycling. Function must follow form.

The primary issue to address is the transport bottleneck caused by rGO restacking. If graphene oxide (GO) is simply mixed with the sulfide precursors and then reduced, the rGO sheets will inevitably aggregate and wrap the nanocubes too densely, blocking the porous walls from the electrolyte. The solution is to use the hollow sulfide nanocubes themselves as structural spacers to keep the rGO sheets separated. This creates a 3D architecture with open channels for electrolyte ingress, a strategy that has proven effective in preventing the restacking of graphene sheets in other composite systems [ID: 211].

To achieve this, I would recommend a strategy based on pre-assembly of the components before the final conversion step. Instead of forming the sulfide and rGO simultaneously from a mixed solution, we should first construct a precursor composite of graphene oxide and the Prussian blue analogue (PBA) nanocubes. GO sheets in aqueous dispersion are negatively charged. By controlling surface chemistry, it's possible to induce electrostatic self-assembly where the PBA nanocubes adsorb onto the surface of the GO sheets. This approach has been used to create well-defined heterostructures with other nanomaterials [ID: 212, 549]. This pre-assembly step is critical; it allows you to control the cube-to-graphene ratio and ensures the cubes are anchored to the sheets, which will prevent them from being completely encapsulated later.

Once this PBA-GO composite is formed, a single, carefully controlled thermal treatment step can be used to achieve both sulfidation and reduction. By heating the composite precursor in a sulfur-containing atmosphere (e.g., with sulfur powder or a low-concentration H2S/Ar flow), the PBA template can be converted into the desired hollow sulfide structure while the GO is simultaneously reduced to rGO. This method of gaseous sulfurization has been shown to be effective for converting MOF templates into porous sulfides while preserving their morphology [ID: 15]. This one-pot thermal conversion of the pre-assembled structure "locks in" the desired architecture: a network of hollow sulfide nanocubes wired together by a conductive rGO scaffold.

This proposed architecture directly addresses the structural bottleneck. The hollow interior and porous walls of the sulfide nanocubes are intrinsically designed to accommodate the large volume changes that occur during the sodiation/desodiation conversion reaction, which is a known benefit of hollow nanostructures in energy storage [ID: 54, 506, 593]. The rGO network then serves as a flexible and conductive matrix that physically holds the active material together. Even if the primary sulfide particles making up the cube walls fracture during cycling, the encompassing rGO web maintains electrical contact and prevents pulverization and electrical isolation of the active material [ID: 258, 362]. This synergy between a hollow active material and a conductive graphene support is key to achieving long cycle life.

Regarding your process concern about preserving the hollow cube geometry, using a pre-formed PBA nanocrystal as a template is a robust strategy. The conversion to a sulfide via anion exchange or gaseous reaction often preserves the template's morphology, as demonstrated in the formation of NiS nanoframes from PBA cubes [ID: 1, 2]. The key is to control the sulfidation conditions (temperature, time, sulfur concentration) to favor a template-mediated conversion rather than dissolution and reprecipitation, which would lead to dense, overgrown particles. Gaseous sulfurization is likely more controllable in this regard than a high-temperature solution-based method [ID: 15].

In summary, to integrate these components effectively, you should focus on creating a specific, pre-defined architecture. Avoid random mixing. A process involving (1) electrostatic self-assembly of PBA nanocubes onto GO sheets to create a spacer-defined precursor, followed by (2) a one-pot thermal treatment for simultaneous sulfidation and reduction, should yield a robust, porous 3D composite. This structure provides the short Na-ion diffusion paths of the hollow cubes, the volume expansion tolerance of the void space, and the long-range electronic conductivity and structural reinforcement of the interconnected rGO network, all while maintaining open channels for electrolyte access.
