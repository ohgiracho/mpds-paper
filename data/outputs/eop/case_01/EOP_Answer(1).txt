[EOP Answer]
Based on the evidence and our understanding of composite electrode engineering, the central problem is balancing the competing needs for ionic and electronic percolation within a densely packed cathode. At active material loadings exceeding 85 wt%, the volume available for both the solid electrolyte (SE) and conductive additive is severely constrained. This creates a classic trade-off: maximizing contact between the cathode active material (CAM) and the SE to ensure Li-ion accessibility can inadvertently isolate the CAM from the electronic network, and vice-versa [ID: 31]. The challenge is therefore not simply about maximizing coverage or filling voids, but about designing an optimal, multi-scale conductive architecture.

Given the constraints of using fixed polycrystalline NCM811 secondary particles and LPSCl via mechanofusion, I propose that a **bimodal particle size distribution for the LPSCl, strategically allocated to both the CAM surface and interstitial voids, offers the most plausible solution.** This approach is a deliberate hybridization strategy, moving beyond the simplistic extremes of prioritizing either surface coverage or interstitial filling alone.

Here is a breakdown of the proposed microstructural design and its justification, compared to alternative geometries:

**Proposed Solution: Bimodal LPSCl Allocation**

This strategy involves using two distinct size fractions of LPSCl powder:

1.  **Fine Fraction (Sub-micron to Nanoscale):** This fraction is intended to achieve a *partial but extensive* coating on the surface of the NCM811 secondary spheres. Its purpose is to create numerous, well-distributed contact points for Li-ion exchange directly at the active material surface. This minimizes the Li-ion diffusion path length on the NCM surface itself, directly addressing the diffusive limitations that become dominant at high active material content [ID: 31, 5].
2.  **Coarse Fraction (Micron-scale):** This fraction, comprising the bulk of the SE volume, is designed to efficiently fill the larger interstitial voids between the NCM secondary spheres. Its primary role is to form the main, low-tortuosity ionic conduction network that bridges the entire cathode thickness, connecting the distributed surface contacts to the separator [ID: 157].

**Justification and Expected Improvements:**

This bimodal approach strikes a critical balance between the three required properties:

*   **Ionic Continuity:** The coarse interstitial LPSCl particles establish the primary, long-range ionic pathways, while the fine surface-adhered particles act as efficient "on-ramps," ensuring the NCM particles are ionically wired into this network. Evidence suggests that using a mix of particle sizes in the catholyte improves electrochemical performance compared to a uniform size, likely by optimizing both packing and connectivity [ID: 345]. This dual-scale network is superior to a purely surface-coated approach where fragile, thin coatings might lack robust long-range connections, or a purely interstitial-filled approach where large areas of the NCM surface would be ionically isolated, leading to dead zones and high charge-transfer resistance [ID: 5].

*   **Electronic Accessibility:** A key aspect of this design is that the surface coverage by the fine LPSCl fraction is *not* a complete, insulating shell. Mechanofusion would create a composite surface on the NCM particles where fine SE and the conductive additive are intermixed. This leaves sufficient exposed NCM surface for direct contact with the conductive additive network, preserving electronic percolation. This is critical, as excessive carbon additives or complete CAM isolation by an electrolyte shell can increase bulk resistance and degrade rate capability [ID: 20, 325, 421]. The coarse interstitial SE particles also help maintain separation between NCM spheres, creating defined channels for the conductive additive to form its own percolating network.

*   **Dense Packing:** From a materials processing standpoint, using a bimodal particle distribution is a well-established method to increase packing density. The smaller particles fill the interstices between the larger particles (in this case, both the coarse SE and the NCM spheres), minimizing void volume and maximizing the overall solid content. This is essential for achieving the target of >85 wt% active material while maintaining structural integrity [ID: 345].

**Comparison with Alternative Geometries:**

*   **Surface-Biased (Fine SE only):** Relying solely on fine LPSCl for a conformal coating would maximize the CAM-SE interface area. However, it presents significant risks. First, the ionic conductivity of sulfide electrolytes can decrease as particle size is reduced and disorder is introduced through extensive milling [ID: 505, 314]. Second, this thin coating could be mechanically fragile and prone to fracture during processing or due to the chemo-mechanical expansion and contraction of NCM811 during cycling [ID: 58, 570]. Third, and most critically, it risks electronically isolating the NCM particles, severely compromising the electronic network.

*   **Interstitial-Biased (Coarse SE only):** Using only large LPSCl particles to fill voids would create robust, low-tortuosity ionic pathways between CAM particles. It would also maximize the potential for direct CAM-to-conductive additive contact. However, it would leave vast portions of the NCM surface ionically disconnected. This creates a severe diffusive limitation, as Li+ ions would need to travel long, tortuous paths across the NCM surface to reach a point of contact with the SE, drastically limiting rate performance and active material utilization [ID: 31].

**Practical Trade-offs and Considerations:**

The success of this bimodal strategy hinges on precise process control during mechanofusion. The energy input must be sufficient to affix the fine LPSCl fraction to the NCM surface and densify the composite, but not so aggressive as to fracture the NCM secondary particles or pulverize the coarse LPSCl fraction, which would negate the benefits of the bimodal distribution [ID: 316]. The relative ratio of fine-to-coarse LPSCl will also need careful optimization. Furthermore, the inherent chemo-mechanical instability of the NCM811-sulfide interface remains a concern; the increased surface area from the fine SE fraction could potentially offer more sites for degradation reactions if not perfectly passivated, although this is a systemic challenge not unique to this geometry [ID: 512, 587].

In conclusion, a bimodal distribution of LPSCl, with a fine fraction for partial surface functionalization and a coarse fraction for efficient interstitial filling, represents the most promising microstructural design. It directly addresses the competing demands of ionic access, electronic continuity, and high packing density, offering a more robust and balanced solution than either extreme geometric allocation strategy.
