[EOP Answer]
Based on my experience with nanostructured carbon hosts for energy storage, the challenge you're facing with Li-Se cathodes is a classic problem of balancing confinement and transport. To effectively immobilize selenium species while maintaining high accessibility for both ions and electrons, a rationally designed, trimodally porous N-doped carbon framework is an excellent strategy. The key is to engineer a hierarchical architecture where each pore size regime serves a distinct, critical function.

From the available evidence, I would propose a framework derived from a metal-organic framework (MOF) precursor, such as a zeolitic imidazolate framework (ZIF). This approach offers a high degree of control over the final carbon's morphology and porosity, and it provides in-situ nitrogen doping directly from the organic linker, which addresses your process concern about reproducibility [ID: 8, 19, 32, 141].

Here is the framework I would design and the rationale behind it, based on established structure-function relationships:

**1. The Trimodal Pore Architecture:**

*   **Micropores (< 2 nm):** These are the primary sites for selenium confinement. The goal is to have a high volume of narrow micropores, ideally in the sub-nanometer range (< 1 nm). These small pores will physically encapsulate selenium and, more importantly, trap the soluble lithium polyselenide intermediates (Li₂Seₙ) through strong capillary forces, directly mitigating the shuttle effect that plagues these systems [ID: 527]. Evidence from Li-S systems, which face a similar shuttle problem, strongly supports this, showing that micropores are critical for suppressing polysulfide diffusion [ID: 124, 261]. For Li-Se specifically, confining selenium within small micropores (e.g., 0.4-0.55 nm) has been shown to be an effective strategy to prevent selenium loss [ID: 527].

*   **Mesopores (2-50 nm):** These larger pores will function as ion-transport highways. While micropores confine the active material, they can also create high tortuosity and impede Li⁺ diffusion. A well-developed network of mesopores provides low-resistance channels for rapid electrolyte penetration deep into the carbon host [ID: 63, 151]. This ensures that the selenium confined within the microporous regions remains electrochemically accessible, which is crucial for achieving high rate capability. Furthermore, these mesopores can act as a buffer to accommodate the significant volume expansion that occurs during the conversion of Se to Li₂Se, thereby preserving the structural integrity of the electrode over many cycles [ID: 527].

*   **Macropores (> 50 nm):** These will serve as electrolyte reservoirs, ensuring the entire electrode is sufficiently wetted, especially at high selenium loadings. They form an interconnected, low-tortuosity network that facilitates bulk ion transport from the separator to the active regions of the electrode, preventing transport limitations on the macro scale [ID: 159, 234].

A framework that successfully integrates these three pore regimes creates a hierarchical system: macropores feed the mesoporous channels, which in turn service the microporous storage sites. This design directly addresses the transport bottleneck you identified.

**2. The Role of Nitrogen Doping:**

Nitrogen doping provides a crucial chemical advantage to complement the physical confinement offered by the pore structure.

*   **Chemical Anchoring of Polyselenides:** Nitrogen functionalities (e.g., pyridinic-N, pyrrolic-N) introduce polar, Lewis basic sites onto the carbon surface. These sites can form strong chemical bonds with the acidic lithium polyselenide species, effectively anchoring them to the host and further suppressing their dissolution into the electrolyte [ID: 115, 141, 497, 527]. This chemical trapping mechanism works in synergy with the physical confinement of the micropores.

*   **Enhanced Electronic Conductivity and Wettability:** N-doping has also been shown to improve the electronic conductivity of the carbon framework and enhance its surface wettability by polar electrolytes [ID: 9, 30, 92]. This leads to lower charge-transfer resistance at the electrode-electrolyte interface and ensures better utilization of the active material.

**3. Proposed Synthesis and Material Choice:**

To realize this trimodal, N-doped architecture, I recommend using a ZIF-derived carbon. A promising route would be the direct carbonization of ZIF-8 nanocrystals.

*   **Synthesis:** ZIF-8 can be synthesized with controlled particle size. The subsequent pyrolysis (e.g., at 900-1000 °C) converts the ZIF-8 polyhedra into N-doped porous carbon (NPC) while retaining the polyhedral morphology [ID: 32, 145]. The intrinsic porosity of the ZIF-8 precursor contributes to the microporosity of the final carbon. The voids between the packed carbon polyhedra and the gas evolution during pyrolysis help generate the required meso- and macroporosity, resulting in a hierarchical structure [ID: 75, 141]. The temperature of carbonization is a key parameter to tune the balance between surface area, graphitization degree, and nitrogen content [ID: 32].

*   **Selenium Loading:** Once the carbon framework is prepared, selenium can be loaded via a standard melt-diffusion process [ID: 422, 518, 527]. The hierarchical porosity will facilitate efficient and uniform infiltration of the molten selenium.

In summary, a MOF-derived, N-doped carbon with a trimodal pore distribution offers the most robust solution to your problem. The micropores provide physical confinement, the N-doping provides chemical anchoring, and the meso-/macropores ensure efficient transport pathways for both ions and electrons. This integrated design directly targets the primary failure modes of Li-Se cathodes—polyselenide dissolution and poor kinetics—while the synthesis route offers the high degree of structural control necessary for reproducible performance.
