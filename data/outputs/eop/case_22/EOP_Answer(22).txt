Based on the evidence, the most effective design strategy is to create a hierarchical, mesoporous tube-in-tube architecture through a shape-preserving thermal conversion of a nickel hydroxide precursor. This approach directly addresses the critical bottlenecks of volume expansion and transport limitations by engineering porosity at multiple length scales while maintaining the integrity of the one-dimensional nanobelt framework.

The core of the problem with high-capacity conversion anodes like NiO is the immense mechanical stress from volume changes during lithiation and delithiation, which leads to pulverization and loss of electrical contact. A dense material simply cannot accommodate this strain. Therefore, the solution must be structural. Our design incorporates voids at two levels: a hollow core within the inner tube and between the inner and outer tubes to buffer the overall expansion, and mesoporosity within the walls of the tubes themselves.

This mesoporous wall structure is the key. Instead of being solid, the walls of each tube should be constructed from interconnected, single-crystalline NiO nanocrystals. This creates a network of fine pores that provides two crucial benefits. First, it allows the primary NiO nanocrystals to expand locally into the void space, mitigating the buildup of mechanical stress across the entire nanobelt and preventing fracture [ID: 81, 127]. This is a well-established principle; porous structures are known to better cushion volume variation during lithium insertion/extraction processes [ID: 81]. Second, these mesopores serve as channels for electrolyte penetration deep into the tube walls, drastically shortening the Li-ion diffusion path length to the active material [ID: 82]. This ensures high rate capability, as seen in similar mesoporous Co3O4 nanobelt systems which demonstrate superior performance even at high current densities [ID: 82].

While creating porosity is essential, maintaining electrical continuity is equally critical. The one-dimensional nanobelt morphology inherently provides a direct, continuous pathway for electron transport along its length, which is superior to the tortuous, particle-to-particle pathways in conventional slurry-cast electrodes [ID: 6]. The interconnected nature of the nanocrystals forming the tube walls ensures local conductivity. While excessive porosity or inactive phases can compromise volumetric energy density and create tortuous paths, a hierarchical structure with optimized porosity balances these factors effectively.

To fabricate such a structure, a precursor-based synthesis route is the most rational approach. Electrospinning, as you are exploring, is a viable method for producing the initial polymer/nickel salt composite fibers, and subsequent calcination can yield the oxide nanostructure. Controlling the temperature during electrospinning is crucial for forming the desired belt-like morphology rather than simple fibers [ID: 166].

However, a more direct and well-documented method for achieving the desired mesoporosity is through the thermal decomposition of a hydroxide precursor. A facile hydrothermal synthesis can first produce ultralong, single-crystalline α-Ni(OH)2 nanobelts [ID: 416]. Subsequent annealing of these precursor nanobelts results in a shape-preserved transformation to porous NiO nanobelts [ID: 416]. This process, analogous to the topotactic conversion seen in Co3O4 systems [ID: 72], uses the release of water during decomposition to generate the internal porosity. This method provides excellent control over the final morphology, ensuring that the advantageous 1D framework of the precursor is retained in the final porous NiO product. By adapting this to a coaxial electrospinning or a sequential hydrothermal process, a porous tube-in-tube architecture can be realized. This design, grounded in empirical evidence from analogous metal oxide systems, provides a robust microstructural solution to the primary failure modes of NiO anodes.
