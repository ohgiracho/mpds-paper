Based on the provided evidence, the fundamental challenge with antimony (Sb) anodes is managing the significant volume expansion (~390%) during sodiation to form Na3Sb, which causes mechanical degradation, pulverization, and loss of electrical contact, ultimately leading to poor cycling stability [ID: 9, 86]. The optimal architecture must therefore be designed as a nanoscale mechanical solution to buffer this strain while ensuring continuous pathways for both ions and electrons, especially at high rates.

The most promising architecture to achieve this without creating non-functional "dead space" is a "yolk-shell" or "nanorod-in-nanotube" structure. This design intentionally incorporates a void space between the active Sb core and a protective carbon/rGO shell. This void is not dead space; it is a critical functional element engineered specifically to accommodate the volume expansion of the Sb phase during sodiation, thereby preserving the integrity of the outer conductive shell [ID: 68].

A prime example of this architecture is the peapod-like Sb nanorod encapsulated within a nitrogen-doped carbon hollow nanotube (Sb@N-C) [ID: 68]. This structure provides a clear blueprint for what you are trying to achieve. It demonstrates superior sodium storage performance, including a capacity of 345.6 mAh/g after an impressive 3000 cycles at a high rate of 2 A/g, which is among the best long-term cycle performance reported for Sb-based anodes [ID: 68]. The success of this design stems from several key features that directly address your experimental bottlenecks:

1.  **Buffering Alloying Strain:** The "substantial inner void space" is the most critical design feature. It gives the Sb nanorod room to expand and contract without exerting mechanical stress on the outer carbon shell, preventing cracking and pulverization [ID: 68]. Other successful approaches also leverage internal voids, such as antimony hollow nanospheres (HNSs), which delivered a high reversible capacity of 622.2 mAh/g [ID: 67]. This confirms that a pre-designed void is the solution to the strain problem.

2.  **Preserving Conductivity and Ion Access:** The shell itself must be robust, conductive, and permeable to sodium ions.
    *   **Composition:** A composite shell of N-doped carbon and rGO is ideal. N-doping has been shown to enhance performance [ID: 15, 36], and a cross-linked carbon network structure provides a highly conductive framework for the fast transfer of both ions and electrons [ID: 12]. The rGO component adds flexibility and ensures excellent long-range electronic conduction, which is crucial for maintaining electrode integrity and achieving high rate capability [ID: 14, 78]. The Sb@N-C structure specifically benefits from its 1D conductive carbon coating [ID: 68].
    *   **Structure:** A dense, thick shell would impede ion transport, as you noted. The architecture should be a porous or cross-linked network rather than a solid wall. The structure in Sb@(N,S-C), for example, is described as a "cross-linked carbon network" that facilitates ion and electron transport while preventing agglomeration [ID: 12]. This directly counters the transport bottleneck.

3.  **Maintaining Structural Integrity:** To prevent aggregation of the active phase, physical confinement is paramount. The nanotube or yolk-shell structure inherently encapsulates the Sb nanoparticles or nanorods, preventing them from agglomerating during cycling [ID: 12, 22, 68]. Furthermore, creating chemical bonds between the Sb and the carbon host, such as the Sb–N bonding suggested in Sb/N-carbon composites [ID: 15] or the Sb-O-C bridges in self-thermally-reduced Sb/C rods [ID: 105], can provide a more robust and intimate contact, further stabilizing the structure.

In summary, the optimal architecture would be Sb nanoparticles or nanorods (~20 nm appears to be a favorable size [ID: 6, 22]) as the "yolk," surrounded by a defined void space, and encapsulated within a "shell" composed of a cross-linked, nitrogen-doped carbon framework interwoven with reduced graphene oxide. This design directly uses the void space as a functional component to manage strain, while the composite shell provides the necessary combination of electronic conductivity, ionic permeability, and mechanical stability to enable high-rate, long-cycle-life performance. While your work uses spray pyrolysis, the syntheses for these high-performing structures are often described as facile and scalable, such as the solvothermal method for Sb@C microspheres [ID: 22] or the bottom-up approach for the Sb@N-C nanorod-in-nanotubes [ID: 68], offering viable routes to producing this advanced architecture.
